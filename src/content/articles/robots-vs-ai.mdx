Everyone drawing the robots-to-AI parallel assumes it transfers cleanly. It does, but only halfway.

Industrial robots ran a 25-year experiment on the US labor market, and economists measured it carefully.
That gives us a real base rate for what happens when a technology directly substitutes for labor at
scale: how concentrated the damage is, how permanent, how well retraining cushions it. On those
mechanics, the AI wave will rhyme with the robot wave.

But the _shape_ of who gets hit is close to inverted. Robots climbed **down** the education ladder and
hit high-school dropouts hardest. AI exposure climbs **up** it, rising with skill and income. So the
analogy is useful for the mechanics of displacement and misleading for predicting the victims. Hover
the bands below; this one picture carries the whole argument.

## The base rate: what robots actually did

The canonical study is Acemoglu and Restrepo's "Robots and Jobs," which tracked US commuting zones from
1990 to 2007. It is the most rigorous natural experiment we have for a machine standing in directly for
a worker, and its results are quotable:

> "According to our estimates, one more robot per thousand workers reduces the employment to population
> ratio by about 0.2 percentage points and wages by 0.42%." ([Robots and Jobs, JPE 128(6)](https://shapingwork.mit.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Robots-and-Jobs-Evidence-from-US-Labor-Markets.p.pdf))

Translate the coefficient into people and it lands harder: on average, one new industrial robot
coincided with about 5.6 fewer workers in its local labor market.

</div>

Three features of this base rate matter for the AI comparison. It was **occupation-specific**: the hit
fell on routine manual work, machinists, assemblers, material handlers, welders. It was
**geographically concentrated**, with Detroit the single most-exposed commuting zone and parts of Texas
reaching 5 to 10 robots per thousand workers. And it climbed **down** the ladder: every education group
lost ground, but workers without a college degree lost far more, and robots showed no positive
complementarity even for workers with advanced degrees. Earlier IT waves had lifted the highly
credentialed. Robots did not.

One honesty note up front. The sharp negative story is most robustly a _US_ result. Graetz and Michaels
(2018) find robots raised productivity and wages across countries while reducing low-skill employment,
and the [German study by Dauth and coauthors](https://docs.iza.org/dp12306.pdf) found robots caused no
net job loss in Germany, only a reshuffling of job composition, plausibly because stronger unions and a
denser safety net absorbed the shock. Hold that thought; it becomes the policy punchline later.

## The part nobody frames well: it is permanent for a minority

The aggregate story is mild. Total US employment moved only a small share. That average hides the thing
that actually matters to a displaced person, which is that for a large minority the loss never reverses.

The scarring literature measures this. Jacobson, LaLonde and Sullivan (1993) followed high-seniority
workers displaced in the 1980s: earnings fell about 30% at separation and were still roughly 20% below
their non-displaced peers 15 to 20 years later. Couch and Placzek (2010) found a similar path, about
33% down immediately and still 15% down six years out.

</div>

Credibility flag, because a technical audience will ask: this literature is mass-layoff-wide, not
robot-specific. It is the base rate for displacement in general, and I am using it as a proxy for what
robot-displaced workers went through. The mechanism is the surprising part. Most of the lifetime cost
does not come from a lower wage on the next job. It comes from the trouble finding stable full-time work
at all: longer spells out of work, more part-time landings.

That is also why "reemployment" is a misleading headline. The BLS Displaced Workers Survey (January 2024) found 65.7% of long-tenured displaced workers reemployed, which sounds like recovery until you
open the number up.

<div class="fx-figure">
  <WhereLanded client:visible />
</div>

Watch the denominators, because the survey uses two. Of the _whole displaced pool_, about a third never
got reemployed. Of the _full-time reemployed subset_, 62% matched or beat their old pay, which means
nearly four in ten took a permanent pay cut. Stack those and the friendly-sounding two-thirds
reemployment rate is hiding a long tail of people who are worse off for good.

The damage does not stay inside the pay stub. Displacement is linked to
[worse physical and psychological health, family disruption, higher divorce rates, and worse outcomes
for the workers' children](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4553243/). And it moves votes.
One study found that a single robot per thousand workers cut local average "career value" by about
$3,900 in 2004 to 2008, and that automation-hit regions
[shifted toward populist and Republican candidates in 2016](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/robots-are-taking-over-low-skilled-jobs-and-changing-votes/).
Displacement is a political event, not only an economic one. Remember that; it is where the AI story
diverges most.

## Where the analogy breaks: the ladder inverts

Here is the section a software or design reader should actually care about. Everything above transfers
to AI as _mechanics_: concentrated, permanent for a minority, poorly cushioned by retraining. What does
not transfer is who stands in the blast radius.

Robot exposure was strongest among high-school dropouts and
[declined monotonically with education](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40821-025-00314-w).
AI exposure runs the other way. The same UK analysis finds AI exposure increasing monotonically across
income percentiles, rising toward the top. Robots concentrated on the middle and bottom of the
distribution; AI exposure climbs toward the credentialed knowledge work at the top.

The obvious objection is that AI and robots are not the same kind of thing, and that is fair. A robot is
a tangible, rivalrous asset bolted to a factory floor; AI is intangible and non-rivalrous, so it can
diffuse in ways a welding arm never could. The authors of the recent
[AI-exposure-index paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.13369) warn explicitly against false analogies for
exactly this reason. The most provocative strand for this audience is Autor's (2024) hypothesis that AI
might _reverse_ the wage polarization robots and IT produced, by putting expert judgment within reach of
less-credentialed workers rather than hollowing out the middle. Nobody knows yet. But the direction of
exposure is already clear, and it points up the ladder, not down it.

## Did anyone fight it?

Yes, repeatedly, for two centuries. The pattern is remarkably stable: labor bargained over the _terms_
of the transition, occasionally struck over its pace, and rarely stopped the technology. It did best
when it had structural power, not when it had a retraining voucher.

</div>

The Luddites are the deep root, and they are misremembered. They were skilled textile workers destroying
frames not out of technophobia but because mechanization destroyed their bargaining position. The same
structure as today, 200 years early. Fast forward and the dockworkers' 1960 Mechanization and
Modernization Agreement traded acceptance of containerization for benefits, and created a two-tier
workforce that prefigured modern two-tier labor while containerization went on to
[eliminate most longshore work](https://www.city-journal.org/article/international-longshoremens-association-dockworkers-ports-trade-technology-unions).
The 1964 East Coast strike closed ports for 18 days and still ended in reduced gang sizes. The work went.

What is genuinely new is the recent hardening. The 2023 Teamsters and UPS contract banned driverless
trucks for its duration, and in 2024 the International Longshoremen's Association threatened to shut East
and Gulf ports demanding a _total_ ban on automating cranes, gates and container moves, framing full
automation as existential rather than tradeable.

This is where two readings collide. The labor-aligned "zombie robot" argument from EPI holds that
"automation causes joblessness" is a myth and the real variable is worker bargaining power over the
terms of transition. Set that against Acemoglu and Restrepo's evidence that the displacement was
measurable and real regardless of how well workers bargained. Both can be true: bargaining power shaped
how much of the loss workers absorbed, and the loss was still there to absorb.

## What companies and governments did, and what worked

Firms had three moves, and often substituted between them: automate, offshore, or both. The "robots and
reshoring" research finds that when production came back to high-wage countries, it came back _more
automated_, not more staffed. "The jobs came back" and "the jobs came back to people" are different
sentences.

On the policy side, the results are humbling for anyone whose instinct is "we will just retrain
everyone." Trade Adjustment Assistance ran from 1962 to 2022 with income support, wage subsidies,
relocation allowances and retraining. One evaluation found participants about $50,000 higher in
cumulative earnings after ten years, but the benefit dissipated over time, and
[retraining worked only when bundled with wraparound supports](https://bipartisanpolicy.org/explainer/what-happens-when-jobs-disappear-a-guide-to-displaced-worker-programs-in-the-u-s/)
like job-search help, counseling and placement. Retraining alone, dropped on a displaced worker, mostly
did not stick.

The counterintuitive punchline is the IMF finding that US regions with more generous unemployment
benefits between 2000 and 2007 saw _smaller_ automation-driven wage losses. The safety net cushioned
displacement more reliably than reskilling did. That is the same lesson the German robot story told from
the other direction. For the AI policy debate, it points somewhere unfashionable: the highest-leverage
intervention is probably income support, not a coding bootcamp.

## The one asymmetry that matters

Put the two waves side by side and the technology is almost a distraction. The decisive difference is
political.

Robots hit a group with weak political voice but strong union structure: blue-collar manufacturing
workers who could strike a port or a plant even as their numbers fell. AI is hitting a group with the
opposite profile, strong political voice and almost no union structure: software engineers, designers,
lawyers, analysts, who can write a viral thread but cannot shut anything down. The robot wave produced
a populist political backlash from people with little bargaining power. The AI wave is arriving at people
with lots of voice and no picket line.

That inversion, not the model architecture, is what will decide how this plays out. And the base rate
offers one uncomfortable prediction under all the uncertainty: the mechanics of displacement, when they
land, are harsh, concentrated and permanent for a minority, and the thing that softened them best was
never retraining. It was a cushion.
