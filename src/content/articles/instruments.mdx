I walked into an exhibition of old instruments in Istanbul expecting to look at some brass for
twenty minutes. I stayed for most of the afternoon. There was a wood-and-brass pendulum clock by
Mustafa Şem'i, a pair of quadrants for telling the time from the sun, a sextant next to an
engineer's treatise on how to use it, a theodolite still in its box with a Constantinople
maker's label, and a hand-drawn quadrant from 1848 with one red line across the grid.

The labels said what each object was. They did not say how it worked, or what people did before
it existed, or what replaced it. Those were the questions I kept asking, so this series is my
attempt to answer them, one interactive drawing at a time.

There are three posts, one per thing being measured: time, latitude, and heights and distances on the ground. Each one
starts with the crudest method that worked and climbs to the current state of the art. The
museum objects are rungs on the way up.

## How precise each instrument was

Before the details, the shape of the whole story. The chart below shows how small an error the
best instrument of each era could hold. The vertical axis is logarithmic, so every gridline is a
factor of ten. A water clock was off by about a quarter of an hour a day. The best optical clock today,
run for the whole age of the universe, would be uncertain by less than a second.

## Sources

- Time: [Pendulum clock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pendulum_clock) (Wikipedia), for "about
  15 minutes per day" before the pendulum, 15 seconds a day after it, and the Shortt clock's
  "better than one second per year".
- [John Harrison](https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/john-harrison)
  (Linda Hall Library), for H4 arriving in Jamaica "only 5 seconds slow" after two months.
- [Caesium atomic clock, 1955](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co855/caesium-atomic-clock-1955)
  (Science Museum Group), for one second in 300 years and the comparison with 1930s quartz.
- [NIST-F2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NIST-F2) (Wikipedia), for "will not gain or lose a
  second in at least 300 million years".
- Aeppli et al., [Clock with 8 × 10⁻¹⁹ systematic uncertainty](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.10664)
  (2024), for the strontium optical lattice clock.
- Angle: Erik Høg, [Astrometric accuracy during the past 2000 years](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.01020)
  (2017), for Ptolemy, Ulugh Beg, Tycho, Argelander, Hipparcos and Gaia.
- [Sextant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sextant) (Wikipedia), for a vernier that "reads to 0.1
  minute", and [Ramsden surveying instruments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramsden_surveying_instruments)
  (Wikipedia), for the great theodolite's "within one arc second".
- The shadow and water clock rung, and the gnomon rung, are order-of-magnitude estimates (half a
  degree is the width of the sun), drawn hollow on the chart.
