In a church in Skopje there is a row of soldier saints painted along the wall. Two of them,
St George and St Demetrius, turn out to be two dates every Turkish speaker knows:
**Hıdırellez** and **Kasım**. Pulling on that thread unravels most of the Ottoman year.

## Two halves of the year

The Ottoman folk calendar, shared by farmers and herders across Anatolia and the Balkans, had
only two seasons:

- **Rûz-ı Hızır**, the days of Hızır: 6 May to 7 November, 186 days. Summer.
- **Rûz-ı Kasım**, the days of Kasım: 8 November to 5 May, 179 days. Winter.

**Hıdırellez** is _Hızır_ + _İlyas_ (the prophet Elijah). Folk belief has the two immortals
meet on earth that night, and spring arrives with them. Hızır is _al-Khiḍr_, "the green
one", whom tradition identifies with the mysterious guide of Moses in
[sura 18](https://quran.com/18/65-82). People tie
wishes to rose bushes, jump over fires, and drop tokens into water. UNESCO listed it in 2017 as
a joint entry of
[Türkiye and North Macedonia](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/spring-celebration-hdrellez-01284);
Skopje's Roma call it **Ederlezi**.

**Kasım** is Arabic _qāsim_, "the one who divides": the day that cuts the year and opens winter.

The halves were practical. Balkan herding contracts ran from St George's to St Demetrius', and
a Serbian proverb says the outlaws (_hajduks_) gather on Đurđevdan and scatter on Mitrovden,
the two saints' days.

## Two saints, thirteen days late

Both are Christian feasts: St George on 23 April and St Demetrius on 26 October. Orthodox churches keep those dates on the Julian calendar, which now runs 13 days
behind the Gregorian one. Converted, they land on **6 May** and **8 November**.

The gap grows by a day every century not divisible by 400. In 2100 it becomes 14, so the
old-calendar St George's Day (Đurđevdan) will slide to 7 May while Hıdırellez, now pinned to 6 May
in Turkey, stays put. Two holidays that were one will drift apart by a day.

## The rest of the folk year

Between the two hinges the year had a few more named stretches, most still on Turkish weather
reports:

- **Erbain** ("forty"): the 40 coldest days, from the longest night on 22 December.
- **Hamsin** ("fifty"): the next 50 days, until the equinox.
- **Cemre** ("ember"): three embers of warmth fall into the air, then the water, then the
  earth, a week apart.
- **Nevruz** ("new day"): 21 March, the Persian new year. The palace handed out a spiced
  paste, _nevruziye_.
- **Eyyam-ı bahur**: the first week of August, the hottest days of the year.

## Three calendars at once

An Ottoman official in 1900 lived on three calendars:

- **Hijri**, lunar, for religion: Muharrem, Ramazan, Zilhicce. It drifts 11 days a year
  against the seasons.
- **Rumi**, solar, for the state. Adopted in 1840 for the treasury, it used Julian days and
  started the year in March. In 1917 it jumped from 15 Şubat straight to 1 Mart to catch up
  with the Gregorian calendar, and in 1926 Turkey adopted Gregorian year numbers too
  ([Rumi calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rumi_calendar)).
- **The folk year** above, for sowing and herding.

The Rumi month names are where today's Turkish months come from.

## Where the month names come from

</div>

The names have three sources.

**Babylon, via Aramaic.** Şubat, Nisan, Haziran, Temmuz and Eylül are Babylonian month names
(_Šabāṭu_, _Nisannu_, _Duʾūzu_, _Ulūlu_) that passed into Aramaic, the lingua franca of the
Near East, and from there into Arabic and Ottoman Turkish
([Turkish months](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_months)). **Temmuz** is the god
_Tammuz_ (Sumerian _Dumuzi_), whose yearly death was mourned in midsummer. Haziran's origin is
disputed. The two **Teşrin**s and two **Kânûn**s came along with them.

**Latin.** Mart (_Martius_, after Mars), Mayıs (_Maius_) and Ağustos (_Augustus_, after the
emperor). In those three slots the Levant kept its Aramaic names instead: Ādhār, Ayyār, Āb.

**Turkish, 1945.** On 10 January 1945 the four double names were replaced:

| Old | New | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Teşrin-i Evvel | **Ekim** | sowing |
| Teşrin-i Sânî | **Kasım** | the winter-opening day |
| Kânûn-ı Evvel | **Aralık** | gap, interval |
| Kânûn-ı Sânî | **Ocak** | hearth, stove |

_Kânûn_ is usually traced to a word for brazier or stove, which makes **Ocak** close to a
straight translation of the name it replaced.

## Who still uses these names

- **Turkey**: the twelve names above, since 1945.
- **Iraq, Syria, Jordan, Lebanon, Palestine**: the Aramaic set for the Gregorian months, from
  _Kānūn al-Thānī_ (January) to _Kānūn al-Awwal_ (December)
  ([Arabic names of Gregorian months](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_names_of_Gregorian_months)).
- **The Hebrew calendar**: Shevat, Nisan, Tammuz, Elul, Tishrei, picked up in the Babylonian
  exile. Its months are lunar, so Nisan falls in March or April.
- **Egypt and the Gulf** use Latin-style names (_Yanāyir_, _Fibrāyir_); **Algeria and
  Tunisia** French ones.
- **Orthodox churches on the Julian calendar**, including the Macedonian, Serbian and Russian
  ones, still keep St George on a date the rest of the world calls 6 May.

So the summer month in Istanbul is named after a Sumerian shepherd god, the winter month after
a day that divided a herder's year, and both halves of that year after two Roman soldiers on a
church wall in Skopje.
