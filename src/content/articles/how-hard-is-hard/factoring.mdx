Multiplying 4,729 by 8,831 on paper takes 16 single-digit products: 41,761,799. Now hand
someone 41,761,799 and ask which two primes made it. Trying 2, 3, 5, 7 and every odd number after
that, they need 2,365 tries before 4,729 divides it. Going back is integer factorization, and it is
much harder than going forward.

## Two directions

For a number with $d$ digits, schoolbook multiplication grows like $d^2$. Trial division has to
try numbers up to $\sqrt{N}$, about

$$
\sqrt{10^{d}} = 10^{d/2}
$$

candidates, so every two extra digits make it ten times slower.

</div>

Trial division is the naive method. The best known one, the number field sieve, is far faster but
still grows faster than any polynomial. With it, a team factored the 250-digit RSA-250 in 2020 in
"roughly 2700 core-years"
([announcement](https://caramba.loria.fr/rsa250.txt)). A common key size today, 2048 bits, has 617 digits.

## A padlock anyone can close

In 1977 Rivest, Shamir and Adleman turned this into a lock. The receiver multiplies two secret
primes $p$ and $q$ and publishes the product $n = p \cdot q$: "the factors p and q will be
effectively hidden from everyone else due to the enormous difficulty of factoring n"
([RSA paper](https://people.csail.mit.edu/rivest/Rsapaper.pdf)). Anyone can use the public
numbers to lock a message, so anyone can close the padlock. Opening it needs a secret number that
is easy to work out from $p$ and $q$, and nobody knows how to get it from $n$ alone. Here is the
paper's own small example, one block at a time.

</div>

In one line: the sender locks with $c = m^{e} \bmod n$ and the receiver unlocks with
$m = c^{d} \bmod n$. A longer message is cut into blocks of two letters, and each block goes
through the same lock and key.

With 2773 you could find 47 by hand in 24 tries. With 617 digits, nobody knows how.

## Where factoring sits

Factoring is in [NP](/articles/how-hard-is-hard/np): a claimed factor is checked with one
division. A "no" answer is just as easy to check, by showing the full prime factorization, so
factoring is also in coNP, and that makes it unlikely to be
[NP-complete](/articles/how-hard-is-hard/np-complete) (Scott Aaronson,
[_P =? NP_](https://www.scottaaronson.com/papers/pnp.pdf), §2.2.4). Telling whether a number is
prime is easy: it is in P ([Agrawal, Kayal and Saxena](https://www.cse.iitk.ac.in/users/manindra/algebra/primality_v6.pdf), 2002).
Knowing that 41,761,799 is not prime does not tell you its factors.

In 1994 Peter Shor showed that a quantum computer could factor in polynomial time
([paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/quant-ph/9508027)). His trick turns factoring into finding a
rhythm: the powers $a^x \bmod N$ repeat every $r$ steps, and once you know $r$,
$\gcd(a^{r/2} \pm 1, N)$ gives the factors. Finding $r$ is the hard part, and it is the part a
quantum computer does well. The figure walks through it for 15.

<div class="fx-figure">
  <Shor client:visible />
</div>

No quantum computer anywhere near large enough exists yet, but this is why cryptographers are
moving to locks that do not rely on factoring.
