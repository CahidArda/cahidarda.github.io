A finished Sudoku takes a minute to [check](/articles/how-hard-is-hard/np) and an evening to
solve. Is that gap real, or have we just not found the clever method yet? Asked for every problem
whose answers are quick to check, this is the **P versus NP** problem:

$$
\mathrm{P} \overset{?}{=} \mathrm{NP}
$$

On 24 May 2000 the Clay Mathematics Institute made it one of seven
[Millennium Prize Problems](https://www.claymath.org/millennium-problems/), with \$1 million for a
solution. Its official description is by Stephen Cook, whose 1971 paper introduced NP-completeness
([Cook, "The P versus NP Problem"](https://www.claymath.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/pvsnp.pdf)).

## Two possible worlds

We know [P](/articles/how-hard-is-hard/p) sits inside NP. Either it fills all of NP, or there is a
gap. If there is a gap, Richard Ladner proved that something lives in it: problems in NP that are
neither easy nor among the hardest ([Ladner, 1975](https://doi.org/10.1145/321864.321877)). Cook
names graph isomorphism as a candidate; [factoring](/articles/how-hard-is-hard/factoring) is
another.

## What would change

In the P = NP world, Cook writes, "an algorithm solving 3-SAT in n² steps could be used to factor
200-digit numbers in a few minutes", which would break the encryption behind online banking. It
would also let a computer "find a formal proof of any theorem that has a proof of reasonable
length". All of that assumes the fast method is fast in practice, not just in theory.

## A letter from 1956

Fifteen years before Cook's paper, Kurt Gödel asked a version of this question in a handwritten
letter to John von Neumann, who was ill with cancer. Take a machine that decides whether a formula
has a proof $n$ symbols long, and let $\varphi(n)$ be the most steps it needs. How slowly can
$\varphi(n)$ grow? In today's terms this bounded proof search is NP-complete. If a machine
managed $\varphi(n) \sim k \cdot n$ or $k \cdot n^2$, Gödel wrote, a mathematician's work on
yes-or-no questions "could be completely replaced by a machine". He also asked about telling
whether a number is prime, which turned out to be [in P](/articles/how-hard-is-hard/factoring).

No reply is known. Von Neumann died in February 1957, and the letter became widely known only in
1989, through Juris Hartmanis ([letter and translation](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~chazelle/courses/BIB/goedel-letter.pdf);
[Hartmanis 1989](https://hdl.handle.net/1813/6910)).

## What experts bet

Most experts think P ≠ NP. William Gasarch has polled them three times; in the 2019 poll, 109 of
124 answered P ≠ NP
([Gasarch, "The Third P =? NP Poll"](https://www.cs.umd.edu/users/gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/pollpaper3.pdf)).

<div class="fx-figure">

</div>

A bet is not a proof. Scott Aaronson's survey explains why the proof is so hard: whole families of
techniques, including every argument that "relativizes", provably cannot settle the question
([Aaronson, "P =? NP"](https://www.scottaaronson.com/papers/pnp.pdf), section 6).

P versus NP asks whether finding an answer can be truly harder than checking it. Almost everyone
bets yes. Nobody has proved it.
