A bathroom floor repeats: find one block of tiles and copies of it cover the room. In 1961 the logician Hao
Wang asked whether that is always so. Take a few square tiles with coloured edges (Wang called them
"plates"), never turn them, and let tiles touch only where their edges match. Can they cover the whole
plane? This is the **domino problem**, and it turned out to have no algorithm at all.

## Wang's searcher

Wang's plan: fill bigger and bigger squares. If some square can't be filled, the answer is no. If a block
turns up whose edges wrap around onto themselves, its copies tile forever: yes. He conjectured (his 4.1.2)
that one of the two always happens, and noted: "If 4.1.2 is true, we can decide effectively whether any
given finite set of plates is solvable."

In 1966 Robert Berger proved it false. His 20,426 tiles (104 in his 1964 thesis) cover the plane but never
repeat, and tiles can copy the run of any program, so a tiling decider would solve the
[halting problem](/articles/how-hard-is-hard/halting). None exists. Raphael Robinson got it down to six
shapes in 1971; in 2015 Emmanuel Jeandel and Michaël Rao found the smallest Wang set, 11 tiles on 4
colours, the third set above.

## Penrose's two tiles

Martin Gardner told the next step in 1977: "In 1973 Penrose found a set of six tiles that force
nonperiodicity. Soon he found a way to reduce them to four, and in 1974 he lowered them to two."

In _The Emperor's New Mind_ (1989) Penrose listed tiling among his examples of "non-recursive
mathematics" and added: "It is not known, for example, if there is an aperiodic set consisting of a single
tile." In 2023 David Smith, Joseph Myers, Craig Kaplan and Chaim Goodman-Strauss found one.

## The finite version

Ask for one $n \times n$ square with its border colours given and the question becomes finite. A searcher
always finishes, though blind search can be slow: with Jeandel and Rao's tiles a 10 by 10 square has

$$
11^{100} \approx 1.4 \times 10^{104} \text{ ways to lay the tiles}
$$

This bounded tiling problem is [NP-complete](/articles/how-hard-is-hard/np-complete): hard, but it
[sits on the map](/articles/how-hard-is-hard/beyond-np). The infinite one is off it, with the halting
problem and Gödel's [unprovable truths](/articles/how-hard-is-hard/unprovable).

<Lesson>

A searcher can always prove that tiles fail. Proving they succeed needs a repeat, and some tiles never repeat,
so no algorithm decides tiling.

</Lesson>
