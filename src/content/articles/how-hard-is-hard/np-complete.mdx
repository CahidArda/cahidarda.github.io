Colouring a map, filling a Sudoku, planning a delivery route. They look like different puzzles,
but each one can be rewritten as any of the others. Such a rewrite is called a **reduction**, and
the hardest puzzles in [NP](/articles/how-hard-is-hard/np), the ones every other NP puzzle can be
rewritten into, are called **NP-complete**.

## A map becomes a formula

Take a map with four countries and three colours. Give each country three switches, one per
colour, and write down two kinds of rules (clauses). Every country gets a colour, and two
neighbours are never both the same colour:

<EqTip note="A_blue means 'A is blue'. The ∨ means 'or', ¬ means 'not'. There are 4 clauses of the first kind and 5 borders × 3 colours = 15 of the second.">

$$
(A_{\text{blue}} \lor A_{\text{amber}} \lor A_{\text{violet}}) \qquad (\lnot A_{\text{blue}} \lor \lnot B_{\text{blue}})
$$

</EqTip>

Colour the map and watch the clauses.

<div class="fx-figure">
  <MapToSat client:visible />
</div>

A proper colouring makes all 19 clauses true, and any way of making all 19 true gives a proper
colouring. So a fast solver for these formulas (the satisfiability problem, **SAT**) also colours
maps fast. In symbols, if $A$ reduces to $B$ in polynomial time:

$$
A \le_p B \;\text{ and }\; B \in \mathrm{P} \implies A \in \mathrm{P}
$$

## The first hard puzzle

In 1971 Stephen Cook showed that "any recognition problem solved by a polynomial time-bounded
nondeterministic Turing machine can be "reduced" to the problem of determining whether a given
propositional formula is a tautology" ([Cook 1971](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~sacook/homepage/1971.pdf)).
In plain words: every NP problem can be rewritten as a logic formula. Leonid Levin found the same
result independently in the Soviet Union. Today it is the Cook-Levin theorem, and it makes SAT
NP-complete.

A year later Richard Karp reduced SAT to 21 classic problems, from clique to knapsack, and noted
that P = NP exactly when SAT is in P
([Karp 1972](https://cgi.di.uoa.gr/~sgk/teaching/grad/handouts/karp.pdf)). Arrows below follow
Karp's paper and Sipser's _Introduction to the Theory of Computation_ (3rd ed., §7.4 to 7.5).

Garey and Johnson's 1979 book _Computers and Intractability_ collected hundreds of these. Thousands
are known now, including Sudoku (Yato and Seta, 2003) and
[Minesweeper](/articles/how-hard-is-hard/minesweeper). A fast algorithm for any one of them would
settle [P versus NP](/articles/how-hard-is-hard/p-vs-np). Nobody has found one, which is why a
problem like the [travelling salesman](/articles/how-hard-is-hard/tsp) is called hard.

<Lesson>

Every NP-complete problem is the same problem in a different costume. Solve one fast and you have
solved them all.

</Lesson>
