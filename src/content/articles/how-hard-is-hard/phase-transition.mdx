Seat ten guests with two wishes, like "Ann not next to Bob", and almost any seating works. Give
them two hundred wishes and you soon see that nothing can. The seatings that take all evening
are somewhere in between. Random logic puzzles behave the same way, and the switch from "easy
yes" to "easy no" is so sudden that it is called a phase transition.

## A cliff in random puzzles

The puzzle is 3-SAT, a version of SAT, the first problem shown to be
[NP-complete](/articles/how-hard-is-hard/np-complete). There are $n$ true-or-false variables
and $m$ rules (clauses), and each rule names three variables and asks for at least one to come out right,
like "$x_1$ or not $x_7$ or $x_9$". What matters is the number of clauses per variable:

$$
\alpha = \frac{m}{n}
$$

I generated 51,000 random puzzles and ran a standard solver on each (DPLL: set a variable,
follow everything it forces, and back up when two rules clash).

<div class="fx-figure">
  <Cliff client:visible />
</div>

With 100 variables, 92% of puzzles at $\alpha = 4$ have a solution and 1% at $\alpha = 5$. The
drop gets steeper as $n$ grows. The solver's work peaks at the same spot: the median puzzle took
40 steps at $\alpha = 3$, 4,740 at 4.4 and 317 at 7.

Mitchell, Selman and Levesque found this in 1992: "the hardest area for satisfiability is near
the point where 50% of the formulas are satisfiable", at about 4.3 rules per variable, and at
4.55 for 20 variables
([_Hard and Easy Distributions of SAT Problems_](https://cdn.aaai.org/AAAI/1992/AAAI92-071.pdf)).
My run puts the 50% point at 4.64, 4.39 and 4.31 for 20, 50 and 100 variables. For huge puzzles,
physicists predict 4.267 ([Mertens, Mézard and Zecchina](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0309020)), still
a conjecture.

## Why the middle is hard

<div class="fx-figure">
  <SearchTrees client:visible />
</div>

With few rules, the first guesses almost never clash, so the solver walks straight down to a
solution. With many, every guess forces so much that a clash comes within a few levels and each
branch dies young. At the threshold, guesses survive for a while and then fail deep down, again
and again. Cheeseman, Kanefsky and Taylor put it this way in 1991: "Really hard problems occur on
the boundary between these two regions"
([_Where the Really Hard Problems Are_](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/1991/0050.pdf)).

"Easy" means easier than at the threshold: Mitchell and colleagues note that for tight puzzles
with enough variables this solver still takes exponential time. And NP-completeness is about the
worst puzzle, while this page is about typical ones, which is part of why
[P vs NP](/articles/how-hard-is-hard/p-vs-np) is subtle.

The hard cases live at the edge, where a solution is barely possible.
