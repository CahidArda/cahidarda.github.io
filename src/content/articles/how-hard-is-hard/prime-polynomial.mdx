Put 26 whole numbers into a polynomial and read the answer. Whenever the answer is positive it is a prime, and
every prime comes out for some choice of inputs. That polynomial exists and fits on half a page. It is a by-product of
Hilbert's tenth problem.

## Hilbert's tenth problem

In 1900 David Hilbert asked: "Given a diophantine equation with any number of unknown quantities and with
rational integral numerical coefficients: to devise a process according to which it can be determined by a
finite number of operations whether the equation is solvable in rational integers."
([Hilbert, tr. Newson](https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/hilbert/problems.html#prob10)).

The answer took 70 years and is no. Davis, Putnam and Robinson (1961) and Matiyasevich (1970) showed that any
list of positive whole numbers a program can print is the set of positive values of some polynomial. A process
for Hilbert's question would then decide the [halting problem](/articles/how-hard-is-hard/halting), which no
program can.

## The polynomial

The primes are such a list. In 1976 Jones, Sato, Wada and Wiens wrote out the polynomial: degree 25, in the 26
letters $a$ to $z$, of the form

$$
(k+2)\,\bigl\{1 - [wz+h+j-q]^2 - [(gk+2g+k+1)(h+j)+h-z]^2 - \cdots - [z+pl(a-p)+t(2ap-p^2-1)-pm]^2\bigr\}
$$

If any of the 14 brackets is not zero, the braces hold 1 minus at least 1, so the output is zero or negative
("e.g., −76", the paper notes). Only when all 14 vanish is the output $k+2$, and they can all vanish only when
$k+2$ is prime.

The brackets check a certificate built on Wilson's theorem,

$$
k+2 \text{ is prime} \iff (k+2) \text{ divides } (k+1)! + 1
$$

with the factorial pinned down by Pell equations such as $x^2 = (a^2-1)y^2 + 1$, whose solutions grow
exponentially.

## Why you cannot just search

For 2, the smallest prime, the paper's construction fixes most letters, and the rest come out of Pell equations
solved exactly for this page. No certificate for 2 has a smaller $a$.

The paper points out that every prime then has "a proof that p is prime consisting of only 87 additions and
multiplications", on numbers like these. Testing primality is in fact easy (it is in P, see
[factoring](/articles/how-hard-is-hard/factoring)); the polynomial is a statement about what equations can
express. The sizes are the point: if the smallest solution of every equation had a computable size limit, you
could search up to it and answer Hilbert. There is no such limit. The same route gives
[unprovable statements](/articles/how-hard-is-hard/unprovable): for a sound proof system, some polynomial
equation has no whole-number solution, and the system cannot prove it.

<Lesson>

A polynomial can list every prime, yet no method decides whether a polynomial equation has a whole-number
solution: the solutions can be too large ever to find.

</Lesson>
