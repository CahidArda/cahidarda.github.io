A maps app finds the fastest way across a city in a blink. It does not try every route: there
are far too many. It follows a recipe whose work grows gently with the size of the map.

Problems with a recipe like that, one whose number of steps grows like a polynomial ($n$,
$n^2$, $n^3$, ...) in the size of the input, form the complexity class **P**, for polynomial time.

## The shortest route

</div>

A town of 6 by 6 crossings already has 1,262,816 routes from corner to corner that never pass
the same crossing twice. At 10 by 10 there are 41 quintillion: checking a billion a second
would take 1,300 years. Edsger Dijkstra's method from 1959
([_A Note on Two Problems in Connexion with Graphs_](https://ir.cwi.nl/pub/9256/9256D.pdf))
never lists them. It settles crossings nearest first, and a settled crossing's distance is
final, so each road is looked at only from its two ends.

## Sorting

  <SortRace client:visible />
</div>

Both sorts are in P: selection sort's $n(n-1)/2$ is a polynomial, merge sort's $n \log_2 n$ is
smaller still. The hopeless way to sort is to try all $n!$ orderings until one is right, which
for 60 items would take longer than [the age of the universe](/articles/how-hard-is-hard/growth).

## Why polynomial is the line

Sipser defines P as everything a Turing machine can decide in $O(n^k)$ steps for some fixed
$k$ (_Introduction to the Theory of Computation_, 3rd ed., §7.2):

$$
\mathrm{P} = \bigcup_{k} \mathrm{TIME}(n^k)
$$

The line is not about any one machine. A polynomial-time recipe that calls another
polynomial-time recipe a polynomial number of times is still polynomial, and every reasonable
model of an ordinary computer can simulate every other one with at most a polynomial slowdown. So
P is the same class on a phone, a supercomputer or a Turing machine.

The class holds more than it seems. Pairing up as many people as possible from a list of who
gets along with whom is in P, shown by Jack Edmonds in 1965
([_Paths, Trees, and Flowers_](https://doi.org/10.4153/CJM-1965-045-4)), who also argued that
"polynomial" is what makes an algorithm good. Testing whether a 300-digit number is prime is in
P, but nobody knew until 2002
([Agrawal, Kayal and Saxena, _PRIMES is in P_](https://www.cse.iitk.ac.in/users/manindra/algebra/primality_v6.pdf)).
An $n^{100}$ algorithm would count too, though in practice the exponents of known algorithms
are small. The [next page](/articles/how-hard-is-hard/np) is about problems where nobody has
found one at all.

<Lesson>

A problem is in P when doubling the input multiplies the work by at most a fixed amount instead of squaring it.

</Lesson>
