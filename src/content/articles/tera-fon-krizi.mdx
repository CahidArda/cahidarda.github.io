On 17 September 2026 Turkey's Sermaye Piyasası Kurulu suspended trading in 131 investment funds
belonging to seven portfolio management companies and started liquidating them. Six days later it
published the number of people holding units in those funds: 455,758
([SPK, via Diken](https://www.diken.com.tr/tasfiye-edilen-131-fon-455-bin-758-kisi-yatirim-yapmis/)).

The question people reach for first is who did this. It is the right question and it has an answer,
with arrests attached. But it is the second question. The first is how a fund can report a
five-year return of 762,829% without anybody having to falsify a single number, and the answer to
that one is not about character at all. It is a loop.

Run it forward and every step is legal. The fund takes in money. It buys more of what it already
holds. Because what it holds has very little free float, the buying moves the price. The fund then
marks its entire position at the new price, which is exactly what the rules require it to do, and
its net asset value jumps by far more than the money that came in, because the new price revalues
every share it already owned. That NAV becomes a published return on a platform anyone can see,
which brings the next saver.

Nobody has to lie for this to work. That is the uncomfortable part.

## The shape of it

Charles Kindleberger spent his career arguing that financial crises are not a grab bag of
one-off disasters but a repeated shape: structural conditions, a *displacement* that starts things
moving, euphoria, distress as the conditions turn, and then revulsion. The NBER's reconstruction of
his model keeps that split between the underlying conditions and the trigger, and puts the exposure
of fraud late, in the cleanup
([Kindleberger Cycles, NBER WP 28411](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28411/w28411.pdf)).

The Turkish episode walks it almost too neatly.

</div>

The structural condition is the one nobody legislated. Turkish hedge funds, *serbest fonlar*, are
restricted to qualified investors, and for individuals the test is mostly a financial-asset
threshold. That threshold was ₺1m. Inflation does not read legislation. A bar set to describe a
narrow professional class quietly became a bar that a great many ordinary savers could clear, and
the category stopped meaning what it was written to mean. SPK raised it to ₺10m on 18 December 2025
([Finansopia](https://www.finansopia.com/ekonomi/spkdan-fonlarda-manipulasyon-duzenlemesi/)), which
is a regulator conceding that the old number had decayed into decoration.

The displacement is TEFAS. Turkey's fund trading platform sits inside every bank app, and when
hedge funds became reachable through it, a product built for a professional audience acquired a
mass-market distribution channel. The thing that changed was not the product. It was who could
reach it, and how little friction stood in the way.

## What they were actually holding

By the end of August 2026, Tera's flagship fund TLY held roughly ₺244bn for 102,616 investors
([fon.org.tr](https://fon.org.tr/fon/TLY)). Here is where that money pointed.

Concentration on its own is not scandalous. Plenty of funds run concentrated books, and a manager
who refuses to diversify is making a defensible bet. What is harder to read charitably is the
*direction* of the concentration. Nearly 29% of the fund sat in three companies chaired by Emre
Tezmen, Tera's founder and main shareholder, two of them the group's own listed holding companies
([Tera Finansal Yatırımlar](https://terafinansalyatirimlar.com/sayfa-yonetim-kurulu-22)). The
largest position of all, at just under a quarter of the fund, was Destek Finans Faktoring, whose
January 2025 IPO was underwritten by Tera Yatırım Menkul Değerler and whose largest single
allocatee was TLY itself, at 15.42% of the offering
([halkarz.com](https://halkarz.com/destek-finans-faktoring-a-s/)).

So the group brought a company to market, its own fund took the biggest slice, and that fund then
spent eighteen months buying more of it with other people's savings. Destek Holding's owner, Altunç
Kumova, later told prosecutors that his company's rise from about ₺15bn at flotation to ₺1.3tn was
<q>anormal bir rakam</q>, an abnormal figure
([NTV](https://www.ntv.com.tr/turkiye/destek-holding-patronu-savciya-anlatti-hisselerin-yukselisi-hosuma-gidiyordu-1742669)).
He is right, and he said it from a position that makes the admission costly.

## Why the NAV was never a price

A fund's unit value is computed by marking its holdings at the last traded price. When the fund is
a small participant in a liquid market, that is a reasonable estimate of what the holdings are
worth. When the fund is the reason the last price is what it is, the mark becomes a statement about
one share rather than about all of them.

<div class="fx-figure">
  <MarkVsExit client:visible />
</div>

This is not an obscure insight. It has a literature and a name. In *Ponzi Funds*, van der Beck,
Bouchaud and Villamaina decompose fund returns into a fundamental component and a self-inflated one
produced by the fund's own price impact, and show that investors allocate capital without being
able to tell them apart, which closes the loop into a feedback cycle that reallocates wealth to
early investors and unravels when the price pressure reverts
([arXiv:2405.12768](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.12768)). Their estimates are sobering for anyone who
assumed this needs extreme conditions: roughly half of a day's price impact reverts within five to
ten days, and they attribute around 2% of all daily fund flows, rising to 8-12% in the most illiquid
decile, to flows chasing a fund's own impact.

Their title is a borrowing. "Ponzi finance" is Hyman Minsky's term, the stage where obligations can
only be met by new financing rather than by the asset's own returns, and Kindleberger built it into
his model decades before anyone could measure price impact in basis points. The 2024 paper is a
1970s intuition with a coefficient attached.

The practical consequence is the one 455,758 people are now living through. A unit redeemed at the
marked NAV is a claim on a price the fund has not tested. The first people out are paid at a number
the last people out will never see, which is the first-mover advantage the FCA described in its own
work on illiquid funds years ago, where the cost of providing liquidity to those who transact is
borne by those who stay
([FCA Occasional Paper 48](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/occasional-papers/occasional-paper-48.pdf)).

## Orchestrated or emergent

Here is the question I started with, and I think the framing is wrong in an instructive way.

The case for emergence is strong and should not be waved away. Nobody coordinated 102,616 people.
They saw a number at the top of a league table and acted on it, which is what the platform was
built to let them do. The loop has no author: it is a property of concentrated funds in thin
markets, and the Ponzi Funds paper finds it running, at smaller amplitude, across ordinary American
ETFs. Most of the money in TLY belonged to people doing something unremarkable in an economy where
holding cash is a guaranteed loss.

The case for orchestration is also strong, and it is specific rather than atmospheric. A crowd does
not select stocks with 14% free float. A crowd does not underwrite an IPO and allocate 15.42% of it
to its own fund. A crowd does not put 29% of a portfolio into companies chaired by one man. SPK's
own regulatory sequence shows it had diagnosed exactly this: on 4 June 2026 it ruled that shares
held through funds controlled by a company's dominant shareholder would no longer count toward free
float
([ParaMedya](https://www.paramedya.com/devami/142075/spkdan-fiili-dolasim-hamlesi-patronun-fonundaki-hisse-artik-serbest-sayilmayacak/)),
and on 28 August it capped hedge fund positions at 8% of circulating shares in companies with low
float
([BloombergHT](https://www.bloomberght.com/spkdan-serbest-fonlara-sert-fren-3786791)). Eighteen
days later the defaults began.

Kindleberger's answer is that this is not a dichotomy, and that getting it backwards is the common
error. He puts the swindles inside the mania rather than before it: the boom creates the appetite,
the appetite creates the fraud, and the panic is what exposes it. His own phrasing, in the chapter
on frauds and the credit cycle, is that crashes are often precipitated by the revelation of some
misfeasance rather than caused by it. John Kenneth Galbraith made the same observation about what
he called the bezzle, the stock of undiscovered embezzlement that swells in good times, when people
are relaxed and money is plentiful, and shrinks in bad ones, when the audits get thorough
(*The Great Crash 1929*, Chapter VIII).

Read that way, the sequence here is legible. The structural conditions made Turkish savers desperate
for real returns. The displacement handed them a product that could manufacture the appearance of
one. The loop did the heavy lifting and required no conspiracy. And inside the euphoria, where
nobody was asking hard questions because the numbers were extraordinary, there was room for
decisions that look very deliberate indeed. The emergent part built the room. What happened in the
room is for the prosecutors.

That is the honest formulation, and it is less satisfying than either pure story. It also matters
legally, because manipulation requires intent and a feedback loop does not, which is precisely why
regulators prefer structural limits over proving what anyone meant. You cannot indict a loop. You
can cap it at 8% of free float.

## Where this sits among its ancestors

The comparison reporters reached for was Woodford, and I think it is the wrong one. Neil Woodford's
fund suspended in June 2019 holding £3.6bn with only 8% of its investments sellable within seven
days, and the FCA's finding was about asymmetric liquidation: managers sold the liquid assets to
meet redemptions, leaving the people who stayed with the illiquid residue
([FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fca-fines-over-woodford-equity-income-fund)).
That is an outflow-side failure. Woodford's unquoted holdings were stale-priced, not price-pushed.
Nothing in the FCA's case says his inflows manufactured the gains that attracted further inflows.

The closer mechanical ancestor is Archegos. Bill Hwang's family office grew from about $10bn of
exposure to $160bn in a year, and the SEC's charge was that he entered swaps to artificially and
dramatically drive up prices in names where his own position was the marginal buyer
([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2022-70)). That is the same physics. The
difference is who held the bag: Archegos blew up prime brokers, institutions that are paid to
manage counterparty risk. Tera's losses landed inside a retail NAV.

The closest Turkish ancestor is older and more uncomfortable. In the 1982 *bankerler krizi*, brokers
sold bank certificates of deposit to the public at a spread and lent the difference onward to
marginal businesses. A November 1981 regulation banned marketing CDs through brokers, the inflow
stopped, Banker Kastelli hit illiquidity, and five banks were eventually taken over with their
liabilities transferred to state banks
([Atiyas, World Bank WPS 147](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/245901468760494639/pdf/multi-page.pdf)).
A rule change cut the inflow; the structure failed weeks later; the state absorbed the resolution
through public banks. Forty-four years on, the position caps landed in August, the defaults came in
September, and the liquidation is being run by İş Bankası and Ziraat. The distribution channel moved
from bank branches to bank apps. Very little else did.

## What happens to the money

The liquidation window was set at three months and extended to six. Assets get sold, proceeds get
distributed pro rata, and what each investor receives depends on what the holdings actually fetch,
not on the last NAV anyone saw. On 1 October SPK authorised interim payments of up to ₺1m per
investor against net investment as calculated by MKK, money market funds first
([Hürriyet](https://www.hurriyet.com.tr/gundem/spkdan-tasfiye-edilen-fonlar-icin-karar-ara-odemeler-yapilacak-43325364)).
Anyone below that cap is whole on paper. Anyone above it waits.

On 30 September, BDDK transferred three investment banks and two factoring companies in the Tera and
Destek groups to TMSF. The containment argument is in the sizing: those three banks are 0.22% of
Turkish banking sector assets
([FinansınGündemi](https://www.finansingundemi.com/haber/tera-destek-ve-hedef-yatirim-bankalari-tmsfye-devredildi/1910039)).
Systemically this is small. For the household that put its savings into a money market fund because
money market funds are the boring option, it is not.

That detail is the one I keep returning to. The product that defaulted first at Tera was not the
762,829% hedge fund. It was a *para piyasası fonu*, the thing people buy instead of a deposit
account, sitting in the same app, two taps away, carrying a name that promises the opposite of what
happened.
