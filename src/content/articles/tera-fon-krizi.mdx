On 17 September 2026 Turkey's capital markets regulator, the
[Sermaye Piyasası Kurulu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_Markets_Board_of_Turkey) or Capital
Markets Board, broadly the Turkish counterpart of the SEC, suspended trading in 131 investment
funds belonging to seven portfolio management companies and began liquidating them. Six days later
it put the number of people holding units in those funds at 455,758
([SPK, via Diken](https://www.diken.com.tr/tasfiye-edilen-131-fon-455-bin-758-kisi-yatirim-yapmis/)).

I knew nothing about any of this three days ago. The coverage I read was mostly about the
investigation and the arrests, which is where the news is. I was curious about something narrower:
whether a thing like this can happen by mistake. Not whether it did here, which I have no way to
judge, but whether a fund can end up reporting a five-year return of 762,829% without anybody
setting out to make that happen.

These are notes from reading into that. It turns out to be a question with a research literature
behind it.

Lira figures below are converted at roughly ₺49 to the dollar, the rate in early October 2026
([Trading Economics](https://tradingeconomics.com/turkey/currency)). The lira lost about 18% against
the dollar over the preceding year, so older lira amounts bought more at the time than that rate
implies.

## Could it happen by mistake?

The research says a fund can inflate its own reported returns without anyone misreporting
anything, provided it holds a large enough share of a thinly traded stock. That is a claim about
what is possible, not about what occurred in Turkey. The diagram below steps through the mechanism
as the literature describes it.

<div class="fx-figure">
  <ReflexiveLoop client:visible />
</div>

Each step is permitted. A fund takes in money. It buys more of what it already holds. Because that
holding has little free float, the buying moves the price. The fund marks its entire position at
the new price, which is what the valuation rules require, so its net asset value rises by more than
the money that arrived, since the new price revalues every share it already held. The NAV becomes a
published return, and the published return attracts the next investor.

So the answer to the question I started with is yes, in principle: the returns can inflate without
anyone misreporting a number. That does not tell you whether it is what happened in any particular
case, including this one.

## The stages Kindleberger described

Reading into this led me to [Charles Kindleberger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_P._Kindleberger),
an economic historian I had not encountered before. His *Manias, Panics, and Crashes* sets out
financial crises as a recurring sequence rather than a series of unrelated events: structural
conditions, a *displacement* that sets things moving, euphoria, distress as conditions turn, then
revulsion. An NBER reconstruction preserves his distinction between underlying conditions and
trigger, and places the exposure of fraud late, during the cleanup
([Kindleberger Cycles, NBER WP 28411](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28411/w28411.pdf)).

The timeline below arranges the documented dates by those stages. A framework this general fits
many episodes, so the arrangement organises events; it does not establish that Kindleberger's
mechanism produced this one.

Two structural conditions are documented. Turkish hedge funds, *serbest fonlar*, are restricted to
qualified investors, and for individuals that status turns largely on a financial-asset threshold.
The threshold was ₺1m, about \$20,000 at today's rate, and it was not indexed to inflation. SPK
raised it to ₺10m on 18 December 2025
([Finansopia](https://www.finansopia.com/ekonomi/spkdan-fonlarda-manipulasyon-duzenlemesi/)).

The second is distribution. TEFAS, the platform through which Turkish funds are bought and sold,
is reachable inside the standard bank apps. When hedge funds became available there, a product
written for a professional audience became accessible to retail buyers.

## What the fund held

Concentrated portfolios are common. What the disclosures additionally show is the relationship
between the fund and its holdings. Just under 29% of the fund sat in three companies chaired by
Emre Tezmen, Tera's founder and main shareholder, two of which are the group's own listed holding
companies ([Tera Finansal Yatırımlar](https://terafinansalyatirimlar.com/sayfa-yonetim-kurulu-22)).
The largest single position, just under a quarter of the fund, was Destek Finans Faktoring, whose
January 2025 IPO was underwritten by Tera Yatırım Menkul Değerler, and whose largest single
allocatee at flotation was TLY itself at 15.42% of the offering
([halkarz.com](https://halkarz.com/destek-finans-faktoring-a-s/)).

Destek Holding's owner, Altunç Kumova, told prosecutors that his company's rise from about ₺15bn at
flotation to ₺1.3tn, a factor of more than 80, was <q>anormal bir rakam</q>, an abnormal figure
([NTV](https://www.ntv.com.tr/turkiye/destek-holding-patronu-savciya-anlatti-hisselerin-yukselisi-hosuma-gidiyordu-1742669)).
These are disclosures and testimony. They do not establish intent, which is what the courts have to
determine.

## Why a NAV is not an exit price

A fund's unit value is computed by marking its holdings at the last traded price. Where the fund is
a small participant in a liquid market, that approximates what the holdings would fetch. Where the
fund's own buying set the last price, the mark describes one share rather than all of them.

<div class="fx-figure">
  <MarkVsExit client:visible />
</div>

This has been studied directly. In *Ponzi Funds*, van der Beck, Bouchaud and Villamaina decompose
fund returns into a fundamental component and a self-inflated component produced by the fund's own
price impact, and find that investors allocate capital without distinguishing between them, which
closes the loop into a feedback cycle that transfers wealth to early investors and unwinds when the
price pressure reverts ([arXiv:2405.12768](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.12768)). They estimate that
roughly half of a day's price impact reverts within five to ten days, and attribute about 2% of all
daily fund flows, rising to 8-12% in the most illiquid decile, to flows chasing a fund's own impact.
Their data is American and European, not Turkish.

The title is a borrowing. "Ponzi finance" is
[Hyman Minsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyman_Minsky)'s term for the stage at which obligations
can be met only through new financing rather than from an asset's own returns.

The consequence for unit holders is a timing effect. A unit redeemed at the marked NAV is a claim
on a price the fund has not tested, so early redeemers are paid at a figure later ones may not
reach. The UK's Financial Conduct Authority described this first-mover advantage in its own work on
illiquid funds, where the cost of providing liquidity to those who transact falls on those who stay
([FCA Occasional Paper 48](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/occasional-papers/occasional-paper-48.pdf)).

## Orchestrated or emergent

Asking whether something could happen by mistake invites the follow-up of whether it did. I cannot
answer that, and at this stage neither can anyone outside the investigation. What can be set out is
what points each way.

Toward the mechanism running on its own: no one coordinated 102,616 investors. The loop requires no
author, being a property of concentrated funds in thin markets, and the *Ponzi Funds* authors find
it operating at lower amplitude across ordinary American ETFs.

Toward deliberate conduct: the disclosures above are hard to produce by accident. Selecting stocks
with low free float, underwriting an IPO and allocating 15.42% of it to your own fund, and holding
29% of a portfolio in companies chaired by one person are decisions, whatever their purpose. The
regulatory record is specific too. SPK ruled on 4 June 2026 that shares held through funds
controlled by a company's dominant shareholder would no longer count toward its free float
([ParaMedya](https://www.paramedya.com/devami/142075/spkdan-fiili-dolasim-hamlesi-patronun-fonundaki-hisse-artik-serbest-sayilmayacak/)),
and on 28 August capped hedge fund positions at 8% of circulating shares in low-float companies
([BloombergHT](https://www.bloomberght.com/spkdan-serbest-fonlara-sert-fren-3786791)). The defaults
began eighteen days after the second rule. Whether those rules triggered the unwind or merely
preceded it is not something the public record settles.

Kindleberger's own treatment places swindles inside a mania rather than before it: the boom
generates the appetite, the appetite generates the fraud, and the panic exposes it. In his chapter
on frauds and the credit cycle he writes that crashes are often precipitated by the revelation of
some misfeasance rather than caused by it.
[John Kenneth Galbraith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Kenneth_Galbraith) made a related
observation about what he called the bezzle, the stock of undiscovered embezzlement that grows in
good times, when people are trusting and money is plentiful, and shrinks in bad ones, when audits
become thorough (*The Great Crash 1929*, Chapter VIII).

On Kindleberger's reading the two are sequential rather than competing: conditions and mechanism
account for the scale, and a mania is also the environment in which misconduct is least likely to
be noticed. That is a framework, not a finding about Tera. Which parts of this case fall where is
what the prosecutions will have to establish.

One consequence is procedural. Manipulation requires intent; a feedback loop does not. That
asymmetry is a reason regulators reach for structural limits, such as a cap at 8% of free float,
rather than for findings about what anyone meant.

## Earlier cases

The comparison that came up most in coverage was Woodford. The mechanism differs. Neil Woodford's
fund suspended in June 2019 holding £3.6bn with only 8% of its investments sellable within seven
days, and the FCA's finding concerned asymmetric liquidation: liquid assets were sold to meet
redemptions, leaving remaining investors with the illiquid residue
([FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fca-fines-over-woodford-equity-income-fund)).
That is an outflow-side failure, and Woodford's unquoted holdings were stale-priced rather than
price-pushed.

[Archegos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archegos_Capital_Management) is closer on the price
mechanism. Bill Hwang's family office grew from about \$10bn of exposure to \$160bn in a year, and
the SEC alleged he entered swaps to artificially and dramatically drive up prices in names where
his own position was the marginal buyer
([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2022-70)). The losses fell differently: Archegos
left prime brokers with the shortfall, whereas the Turkish funds' losses sit inside retail NAVs.

There is also a Turkish precedent. In the 1982 *bankerler krizi*, brokers sold bank certificates of
deposit to the public at a spread and lent the difference to marginal businesses. A November 1981
regulation banned marketing CDs through brokers, the inflow stopped, Banker Kastelli hit
illiquidity, and five banks were eventually taken over with their liabilities transferred to
state-owned banks
([Atiyas, World Bank WPS 147](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/245901468760494639/pdf/multi-page.pdf)).
The sequence in 1982 was a rule change, then failure, then resolution through public banks. In 2026
the position caps came in August, the defaults in September, and the liquidation is being run by İş
Bankası and Ziraat, two large state-linked banks.

## What happens to the money

The liquidation window was set at three months and extended to six. Assets are sold and proceeds
distributed pro rata, so what each investor receives depends on what the holdings fetch rather than
on the last published NAV. On 1 October SPK authorised interim payments of up to ₺1m per investor,
about \$20,000, against net investment as calculated by MKK, Turkey's central securities
depository, with money market funds paid first
([Hürriyet](https://www.hurriyet.com.tr/gundem/spkdan-tasfiye-edilen-fonlar-icin-karar-ara-odemeler-yapilacak-43325364)).

On 30 September the
[Banking Regulation and Supervision Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banking_Regulation_and_Supervision_Agency)
(BDDK) transferred three investment banks and two factoring companies in the Tera and Destek groups
to the
[Savings Deposit Insurance Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savings_Deposit_Insurance_Fund_of_Turkey)
(TMSF), Turkey's bank resolution body and rough counterpart of the FDIC. The three banks hold 0.22%
of Turkish banking sector assets
([FinansınGündemi](https://www.finansingundemi.com/haber/tera-destek-ve-hedef-yatirim-bankalari-tmsfye-devredildi/1910039)),
which is the basis for official statements that the episode is contained at the system level.

One detail is worth recording. The first Tera product to default was not the hedge fund with the
762,829% five-year return. It was a *para piyasası fonu*, a money market fund, the product savers
use as a deposit substitute.
