You meet a stranger on a train and find a friend in common within ten minutes. It happens too often to be luck.

In 1967 Stanley Milgram asked 296 people in Nebraska and Boston to pass a letter, hand to hand through
first-name acquaintances, to a stockbroker near Boston. 64 letters arrived, after 5.2 intermediaries on average
([Travers and Milgram, *Sociometry* 32(4), 1969](https://doi.org/10.2307/2786545)). In 2016 Facebook measured
3.57 degrees of separation between its 1.59 billion users
([Facebook Research, "Three and a half degrees of separation"](https://research.fb.com/blog/2016/02/three-and-a-half-degrees-of-separation/)).

## Cliques plus a few shortcuts

Real friendships are clustered: your friends tend to know each other. Count it for one person $i$ with $k_i$
friends:

<EqTip note="The share of pairs of i's friends who are friends with each other. 1 means a complete clique, 0 means none of them know each other.">

$$
C_i = \frac{\text{links among } i\text{'s friends}}{\binom{k_i}{2}}
$$

</EqTip>

A world made only of tight local circles has high $C$, but a message has to crawl around it. Duncan Watts and
Steven Strogatz showed that rewiring a few links at random fixes the distance without breaking the circles
([Watts and Strogatz, *Nature* 393, 1998](https://doi.org/10.1038/30918)):

The shortcuts are rare, so most friends of friends are still friends. But each one spans the ring, and every
chain can borrow it. Distance drops long before clustering does.

## The strength of weak ties

The shortcuts are usually acquaintances. Mark Granovetter asked people who had found a job through a personal
contact how often they saw that contact: 16.7% often, 55.6% occasionally, 27.8% rarely
([Granovetter, "The Strength of Weak Ties", *American Journal of Sociology* 78(6), 1973](https://doi.org/10.1086/225469)).
Close friends know what you already know.

Each thin line here is a local bridge: its two ends have no friend in common, so it is the only short way between
two circles. Easley and Kleinberg cover clustering and weak ties in chapter 3, and the small world in chapter 20 of
[*Networks, Crowds, and Markets*](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/home/kleinber/networks-book/networks-book.pdf).

<Lesson>

Close friends make the world clustered, acquaintances make it small, and new information travels over the acquaintances.
