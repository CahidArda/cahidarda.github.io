Picture a ballot where you write two names and both count the same. The name with the most votes becomes
President, the runner-up Vice President. That was the rule for the first four presidential elections: electors
"vote by Ballot for two Persons" ([Article II, Section 1](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/constitution-transcript)),
with no line saying which name is meant for which job. Candidates fight over where to stand ([Why Rivals Huddle](/articles/logic-of-crowds/hotelling)); this is
about the electors, and how they cast their votes.

## Allies in the way of each other

In 1789 everyone expected Washington, and the Federalists wanted John Adams second. If every elector wrote
Washington + Adams, the two would tie. Worse, a few electors leaving Washington off
would put Adams first. Alexander Hamilton: "Every body is aware of that defect in the constitution which renders
it possible that the man intended for Vice President may in fact turn up President"
([Hamilton to James Wilson, 25 Jan 1789](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-05-02-0075)).

With $a$ electors writing Washington + Adams and $k$ writing Adams + someone else:

<EqTip note="Washington loses one vote for each elector who leaves him off; Adams gains one. Set A ≥ W and the 69s cancel into a + 2k ≥ 69. Each defector counts twice.">

$$
W = 69 - k \qquad A = a + k \qquad A \ge W \iff a + 2k \ge 69
$$

</EqTip>

Every Federalist elector is in a [best-response](/articles/logic-of-crowds/nash) puzzle: writing Adams is right
only if enough of the others do not.

## Throwing votes away

Hamilton's answer was "to throw away a few votes say 7 or 8": two in Connecticut, two in New Jersey, three or
four in Pennsylvania. Connecticut reported back: "Our Votes were given agreeably to your wishes
Washington 7—Adams 5. Governor Huntington 2."
([Wadsworth to Hamilton, Feb 1789](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-05-02-0079)).
Easley and Kleinberg call this "strategic misreporting of preferences"
([_Networks, Crowds, and Markets_, ch. 23.4](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/home/kleinber/networks-book/networks-book.pdf)).

<div class="fx-figure">
  <SecondBallots client:visible />
</div>

The count went far past 7 or 8. Not every stray vote was a throwaway: Clinton's 3 came from Anti-Federalists, and
for others, such as Jay's 9, the letters do not settle it. Georgia's are clear: Madison heard its second votes were
"all thrown away on individuals of the State"
([Madison to Washington, 5 Mar 1789](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Madison/01-12-02-0004)).
Adams, with 34, took it badly: "Throwing Away a Vote is betraying a Trust, it is a Breach of Honour, it is a
Perjury" ([Adams to John Trumbull, 25 Apr 1790](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Adams/06-20-02-0193)).

## 1800: nobody threw one away

By 1800 there were parties with running mates. One Rhode Island Federalist wrote John Jay instead of Charles
Pinckney, which kept Adams (65) above Pinckney (64). No Republican elector did the same for Aaron Burr, so
Jefferson and Burr tied at 73 ([National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/electoral-college/1800)), and the
House had to choose.

<div class="fx-figure">
  <HouseBallots client:visible />
</div>

The fix was the 12th Amendment, ratified June 15, 1804: electors "name in their ballots the person voted for as
President, and in distinct ballots the person voted for as Vice-President"
([Amendment XII](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/amendments-11-27)).

<Lesson>

When two votes count the same, allies have to throw some away on purpose. In 1800 they did not, and it took the
House 36 ballots.

</Lesson>
