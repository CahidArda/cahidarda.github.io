Why do two rival phone shops open next door to each other, and why do two candidates end up sounding the same?
Harold Hotelling asked this in 1929 with a beach and two ice-cream sellers
([Hotelling, "Stability in Competition", _Economic Journal_ 39(153)](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2224214)).

## Two carts on a beach

Sunbathers are spread evenly along a 1 km beach. Each walks to the nearer cart. The prices are fixed and
equal, so each cart wants only one thing: more of the beach on its side.

<div class="fx-figure">
  <Beach client:visible />
</div>

The carts start where walkers would put them, at the quarter marks. Then each notices that a step toward
the rival steals customers from the middle while losing none at the back. Step by step they meet at the
centre, where neither can gain by moving. The split is still 50/50, but the average walk has doubled:

<EqTip note="With customers spread evenly and carts at 1/4 and 3/4, nobody walks more than 1/4 of the beach and the average is 1/8. With both carts at 1/2, people at the ends walk half the beach and the average is 1/4.">

$$
\bar d_{\text{spread}} = \tfrac18 \;(125\text{ m}) \qquad \bar d_{\text{huddled}} = \tfrac14 \;(250\text{ m})
$$

</EqTip>

## Two candidates on a spectrum

Swap the beach for a left-right axis, sunbathers for voters, and carts for candidates. Each voter picks the
closer candidate. Anthony Downs made this argument in 1957
([Downs, "An Economic Theory of Political Action in a Democracy", _Journal of Political Economy_ 65(2)](https://doi.org/10.1086/257897)).
Voters are rarely spread evenly, so pick a crowd:

<div class="fx-figure">
  <Ballot client:visible />
</div>

Both candidates end at the voter who splits the electorate in half, the median $m$:

$$
F(m) = \tfrac12
$$

where $F(x)$ is the share of voters to the left of $x$. A candidate standing anywhere else hands the rival
more than half the votes. The mean does not matter, and in a polarized crowd the winning spot is one where
almost nobody lives.

## Add a third cart

The huddle needs exactly two players. With three carts on a line there is no spot where everyone is content:
whoever ends up in the middle is squeezed to a sliver and jumps over a neighbour, which squeezes someone else.

<div class="fx-figure">
  <ThreeCarts client:visible />
</div>

A caveat: Hotelling's own model let the sellers set prices too, and later work showed the clustering result
does not survive that version ([d'Aspremont, Gabszewicz and Thisse, _Econometrica_ 47(5), 1979](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1911955)).
With fixed prices, as here, the pull to the middle holds.

<Lesson>

When rivals compete only on location, each moves next to the other, and the customers pay for it in extra walking.

</Lesson>
