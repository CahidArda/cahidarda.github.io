A city builds a new road to ease traffic. A week later, everyone's commute is longer. Nobody
made a mistake: each driver took the fastest route they could find. This is Braess's paradox.

## A free shortcut

This is the example from chapter 8 of Easley and Kleinberg's
[_Networks, Crowds, and Markets_](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/home/kleinber/networks-book/networks-book.pdf).
4000 drivers go from A to B. Two roads are wide highways that always take 45 minutes. The other
two are narrow: with $x$ cars on them, they take $x/100$ minutes.

<EqTip note="Each route is one narrow road plus one highway. Split evenly, x = 2000 on each narrow road.">

$$
T_{\text{A-C-B}} = \frac{x_{AC}}{100} + 45 = \frac{2000}{100} + 45 = 65 \text{ min}
$$

</EqTip>

Now open a short, fast bridge from C to D. Crossing it takes about 0 minutes, however many cars use it. Watch the drivers react.

<div class="fx-figure">
  <NewRoad client:visible />
</div>

The first drivers to take the bridge save more than 20 minutes. Each later switch also helps the
driver who makes it, and slows down everyone else a little. The process stops only when all 4000
drivers are on A-C-D-B: $40 + 0 + 40 = 80$ minutes, because either old route would now take 85. Every
driver is 15 minutes slower than before the road existed. This is Braess's paradox, first described
by the mathematician Dietrich Braess in 1968.

## What selfishness costs

The simplest case has two roads and nothing to build. One road always takes an hour. The other
takes $x$ hours when a fraction $x$ of the drivers use it. The average trip is

<EqTip note="x of the drivers take x hours each; the rest take 1 hour each. Smallest at x = 1/2, where it equals 3/4.">

$$
C(x) = x \cdot x + (1 - x) \cdot 1
$$

</EqTip>

<div class="fx-figure">
  <SelfishOptimal client:visible />
</div>

Splitting traffic in half gives the best average, 45 minutes. But half the drivers then sit on a road
that is twice as slow as the other, so they switch, until everyone takes 60 minutes. The ratio
between the two, 4/3, is the worst it can get: Roughgarden and Tardos proved that when travel times
grow linearly with traffic, selfish routing never costs more than 4/3 of the best possible
([_How Bad Is Selfish Routing?_](https://www.timroughgarden.org/papers/routing.pdf), 2002).

<Lesson>

When everyone picks their own fastest route, more road can mean slower trips for all.

</Lesson>
