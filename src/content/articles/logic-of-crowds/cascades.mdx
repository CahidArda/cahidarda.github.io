Two restaurants, side by side. One is full, one is empty. You walk into the full one, even if a
friend told you the empty one is better. That can be the smart move, and it is also how a whole
street ends up wrong.

## Ignoring what you know

The classroom version is an urn ([Easley & Kleinberg, ch. 16](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/home/kleinber/networks-book/networks-book.pdf)).
It holds either two blue balls and one red, or two red and one blue, with even odds. People
come up one at a time, draw a ball in private, and announce their guess out loud.

Each private ball is as strong as any other, so a guess that reveals a ball counts as one vote.
Once one colour leads the public guesses by two, your single ball can only cut that lead to one, so
you go with the crowd. From then on every guess repeats the leader and adds no information. The
line is stuck.

</div>

The first two balls decide the line. Both wrong happens with probability
$\frac13 \cdot \frac13 = \frac19$, both right with $\frac49$, and a split puts you back at zero. So in a
long line the crowd locks onto the wrong urn with probability

$$
\frac{1/9}{1/9 + 4/9} = \frac15
$$

Twelve people who pooled their balls would be wrong only about 12% of the time. The information
was there. The order of speaking threw it away. That also makes a cascade fragile: one person who
shows their ball in public can break it.

## Switching when your friends switch

A new messaging app, a new slang word, a new farming method. Here the choice is a coordination
game with each friend ([Easley & Kleinberg, ch. 19](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/home/kleinber/networks-book/networks-book.pdf)):
you both get $a$ if you both use the new thing, $b$ if you both stick with the old one, and nothing
if you differ. If a fraction $p$ of your friends have switched, switching pays when $pa \ge (1-p)b$:

<EqTip note="q is the share of your friends that must switch before you do. A better new thing (bigger a) lowers it.">

$$
p \ \ge\ q = \frac{b}{a+b}
$$

</EqTip>

<div class="fx-figure">
  <IdeaSpreads client:visible />
</div>

The idea runs through the loose town and the thin chains, then stops at the group in the corner.
Its members talk mostly to each other: each keeps more than $1-q$ of their friends inside the group,
so the outside can never reach a share of $q$. Easley and Kleinberg prove this is the only
thing that can stop it: the idea fails to reach everyone exactly when the rest of the network
contains a cluster of density greater than $1-q$.

<Lesson>

Crowds copy actions, not reasons. A cascade can lock in on two people's luck, and a new idea
spreads until it meets a group that mostly listens to itself.

</Lesson>
