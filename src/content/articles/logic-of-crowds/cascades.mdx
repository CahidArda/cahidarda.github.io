Two restaurants side by side, one full, one empty. You pick the full one, even though a friend
said the empty one is better. That can be the smart move. It is also how a whole street ends up wrong: an information cascade.

## Ignoring what you know

The classroom version is an urn ([Easley & Kleinberg, ch. 16](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/home/kleinber/networks-book/networks-book.pdf)).
It holds either two blue balls and one red, or two red and one blue, with even odds. People
come up one at a time, draw a ball in private, and announce their guess out loud.

Every ball is equally strong evidence. Once one colour leads the public guesses by two, your one
ball can only cut that lead to one, so you follow the crowd. So does everyone after you, and their
guesses add no information.

It comes down to pairs of revealed balls. Both wrong happens with probability
$\frac13 \cdot \frac13 = \frac19$, both right with $\frac49$, and a split puts you back at zero. So in a
long line the crowd locks onto the wrong urn with probability

$$
\frac{1/9}{1/9 + 4/9} = \frac15
$$

Pooling all twelve balls would be wrong only 12% of the time. The information was there; the order
of speaking threw it away. It also makes a cascade fragile: it rests on two balls of evidence, so a
couple of balls shown in public can tip it.

## Switching when your friends switch

A new app or a new slang word is different: it pays to match your friends. Each friendship is a coordination game ([Easley & Kleinberg, ch. 19](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/home/kleinber/networks-book/networks-book.pdf)):
you both get $a$ if you both use the new thing, $b$ if you both stick with the old one, and nothing
if you differ. If a fraction $p$ of your friends have switched, switching pays when $pa \ge (1-p)b$:

<EqTip note="q is the share of your friends that must switch before you do. A better new thing (bigger a) lowers it.">

$$
p \ \ge\ q = \frac{b}{a+b}
$$

</EqTip>

<div class="fx-figure">
  <IdeaSpreads client:visible />
</div>

The idea sweeps the loose town, then stops at the group in the corner. Each member keeps more than
$1-q$ of their friends inside it, so the outside never reaches a share of $q$. Easley and Kleinberg
prove this is the only obstacle: an idea fails to reach everyone exactly when the rest of the network
contains a cluster of density greater than $1-q$.

<Lesson>

A crowd sees what people did and never why. A cascade can lock in on two people's luck, and a new idea
spreads until it meets a group that mostly listens to itself.

</Lesson>
