On eBay you type the most you would pay, and the site raises your bid only as far as it needs to beat the
next bidder. So the winner pays roughly the second-highest price. That one rule changes how you should bid. Economists call it a second-price, or Vickrey, auction.

## Pay the second price

Everyone writes a bid in a sealed envelope. The highest bid wins, but pays the **second-highest** bid
(William Vickrey's design, [_Journal of Finance_, 1961](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1961.tb02789.x)).
The item is worth $v$ to you, you bid $b$, and the best rival bid is $r$:

$$
\text{gain} = \begin{cases} v - r & \text{if } b > r \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
$$

Your bid never sets your price. It only decides whether you win. The map below colours every pair of
(your bid, rival bid):

  <BidPlot client:visible />
</div>

Bidding above your value adds only red: cases where you win and pay more than the item is worth. Bidding
below adds only grey: wins you wanted and gave away. The line $b = v$ touches neither, so bidding your
value is never worse than any other bid, whatever the others do. Game theorists call this a weakly
dominant strategy ([Easley & Kleinberg, _Networks, Crowds, and Markets_, ch. 9 "Auctions", section 9.4](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/home/kleinber/networks-book/networks-book.pdf)).

Flip the plot to first price and the honest line earns exactly zero everywhere. If you pay what you bid,
you have to bid less than the item is worth.

## How much to shade

Say $n$ bidders have values spread evenly between \$0 and \$100. In a first-price auction the equilibrium
bid is

<EqTip note="If everyone else bids this fraction of their value, a bid b wins when all n−1 rivals bid below it. Maximising (v − b) times that chance gives b = (n−1)v/n.">

$$
b(v) = \frac{n-1}{n}\, v
$$

</EqTip>

Two bidders each bid half their value; with ten, each bids 90% of it. More rivals, less shading
([Easley & Kleinberg, section 9.7](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/home/kleinber/networks-book/networks-book.pdf)).
So which format earns the seller more? Run the auctions:

<div class="fx-figure">
  <Revenue client:visible />
</div>

Neither. In both formats the bidder with the highest value wins, and the seller expects the same revenue,
$\frac{n-1}{n+1}$ of the top possible value. This is revenue equivalence. The first-price seller gets a
shaded top bid, the second-price seller gets an honest second bid, and on average the two are equal.

<Lesson>

When the price you pay does not depend on your own bid, the best bid is the truth.

</Lesson>
