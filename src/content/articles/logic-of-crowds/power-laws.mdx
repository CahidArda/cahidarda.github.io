Nobody is ten times taller than average. Plenty of web pages have ten thousand times the average number of
links. Heights come from many small independent causes. Popularity comes from people looking at what other
people chose. That feedback, called preferential attachment, produces a power law.

## Luck, amplified

In 2006 Matthew Salganik, Peter Dodds and Duncan Watts let 14,341 people listen to 48 songs by unknown bands
and download the ones they liked. Some saw only band and song names. The rest were split into eight separate
"worlds", each showing its own download counts. The same song could be a hit in one world and ignored in
another ([Salganik, Dodds and Watts, _Science_ 311, 2006](https://pdodds.w3.uvm.edu/research/papers/years/2006/salganik2006a.pdf)).

## A straight line on a log-log plot

Now let the world grow. Each new web page links to one older page: with probability $p$ a page picked at
random, otherwise it copies the link of a random older page. Copying a random link is the same as choosing a
page in proportion to the links it already has, so the rich get richer. This is the model in chapter 18 of
[Easley and Kleinberg, _Networks, Crowds, and Markets_](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/home/kleinber/networks-book/networks-book.pdf),
and it predicts a power law:

<EqTip note="P(k) is the share of pages with exactly k in-links. Taking logs of both sides turns the power into a slope, which is why the points fall on a straight line on log-log axes.">

$$
P(k) \propto k^{-\alpha}
$$

$$
\log P(k) = -\alpha \log k + c
$$

</EqTip>

<div class="fx-figure">
  <BellVsPower client:visible />
</div>

For the copying model the slope is $\alpha = 1 + \frac{1}{1-p}$, so it sits a little above 2 when most
links are copied. A crawl of 200 million real pages found 2.1
([Broder et al., "Graph structure in the Web", 2000](https://www.math.cmu.edu/users/ctsourak/T797003/Readings/broder00bowtie.pdf)). The simulated slope comes out
slightly shallower than the formula, which describes the limit of an infinitely large web.

## The long tail

Rank everything by popularity and the two worlds look nothing alike. A few pages hold most of the links,
while most pages share a thin tail. This is the same attention that [PageRank](/articles/logic-of-crowds/pagerank)
measures and that [cascades](/articles/logic-of-crowds/cascades) pass along.
