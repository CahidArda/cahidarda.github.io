Most people would happily live on a street where they are in the minority. They would just
rather not be the only one. Thomas Schelling showed in 1971 that this mild wish is enough to split
a city in two ([Schelling, "Dynamic Models of Segregation"](https://doi.org/10.1080/0022250X.1971.9989794)).

## One resident's rule

Put two kinds of people on a grid with a few empty lots. Each resident looks at the occupied cells
around her and stays if enough of them are her own kind
([Easley & Kleinberg, section 4.5](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/home/kleinber/networks-book/networks-book.pdf)):

<EqTip note="Count only occupied neighbouring cells. t is the bar: t = 34% means 3 alike out of 8 is enough.">

$$
\text{stay if}\quad \frac{\#\,\text{alike neighbours}}{\#\,\text{occupied neighbours}} \ \ge\ t
$$

</EqTip>

## The whole city

Now let everyone follow that rule. An unhappy resident moves to a random empty lot. Each move can
leave a few former neighbours one short of their bar, and they move too.

<div class="fx-figure">
  <City client:visible />
</div>

The city starts with about half of everyone's neighbours alike, which is what a coin flip gives.
At a bar of 34% it settles with roughly three quarters alike. Nobody asked for that. Each move is
small and reasonable, and the pattern is something no single resident chose.

## Asking for a little, getting a lot

Run the city to rest for every bar and compare what people asked for with what they ended up with.

<div class="fx-figure">
  <TippingCurve client:visible />
</div>

The gap opens early. Even a bar of 30% ends around 75% alike. The curve only falls back when the bar is so strict that nobody can settle at all.

<Lesson>

You cannot read intentions off a map. A city can sort itself sharply even when every resident
would be content as a minority.

</Lesson>
