Two roommates share a kitchen. Each would rather the other one did the dishes, and so the sink
fills up. Both would prefer a clean kitchen, and neither can get one alone.

## One round

Each player either cooperates or defects. The payoffs are temptation $T$, reward $R$, punishment
$P$ and sucker $S$, and two inequalities make it a dilemma
([Axelrod and Hamilton, 1981](https://public.websites.umich.edu/~axe/research/Axelrod%20and%20Hamilton%20EC%201981.pdf),
Fig. 1):

$$
T > R > P > S \qquad 2R > T + S
$$

<div class="fx-figure">
  <OneShot client:visible />
</div>

Defect is a dominant strategy, so mutual defection is the only
[Nash equilibrium](/articles/logic-of-crowds/nash). Both players act sensibly and both end up worse
off.

## Many rounds

Now suppose you will probably meet again: after each round, another one follows with probability
$\delta$. Say your partner cooperates until you defect once, and then never cooperates again.
Cheating pays $T$ once and $P$ forever after, so it stops paying when

<EqTip note="Cooperating forever is worth R/(1-δ). Cheating is worth T + δP/(1-δ). The first is larger exactly when δ is at least (T-R)/(T-P). With T=5, R=3, P=1 that is 1/2.">

$$
\delta \ge \frac{T - R}{T - P} = \frac{5 - 3}{5 - 1} = \frac{1}{2}
$$

</EqTip>

## The tournament

In the late 1970s the political scientist Robert Axelrod asked game theorists to submit programs for a
round-robin tournament of the repeated game. Of the 14 entries, Tit for Tat won: cooperate first,
then copy the opponent's last move. It won again in a second, larger tournament
([Axelrod and Hamilton, 1981](https://public.websites.umich.edu/~axe/research/Axelrod%20and%20Hamilton%20EC%201981.pdf)).

<div class="fx-figure">
  <Tournament client:visible />
</div>

In this field Tit for Two Tats edges ahead of Tit for Tat, but the split is the one Axelrod found:
every **nice** program (one that never defects first) finishes above every program that is not. He
named two more traits of the winners: **retaliatory** (answer a defection) and **forgiving** (go back
to cooperating afterwards).

## Evolution

Now let strategies breed: a strategy's share grows when it scores above average. That is the replicator equation, where $x_i$ is the share of strategy $i$, $f_i$ its
average payoff and $\bar f$ the population mean:

$$
\dot x_i = x_i \left( f_i - \bar f \right)
$$

Players also make mistakes: with some small probability, a move comes out the opposite of what
the strategy intended.

<div class="fx-figure">
  <Evolution client:visible />
</div>

Mistakes are hard on Tit for Tat: one slip between two copies starts an echo of retaliation.
Pavlov (win-stay, lose-shift) gets back to cooperating in two rounds, which is why it beat Tit for
Tat in [Nowak and Sigmund's 1993 simulations](https://www.nature.com/articles/364056a0). I
[replicated that paper in Java](/articles/prisoners-dilemma-rl) a few years ago and saw the same
rise of Pavlov, and its collapse late in the run.

<Lesson>

Defection wins any single round. Cooperation comes back when players are likely to meet again and
answer kindness with kindness and betrayal with betrayal.

</Lesson>
