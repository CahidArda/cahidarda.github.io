The case that stopped me in this part of the exhibition held almost nothing: a few brass
weights on threads and some small brass triangles, hung in front of a blown-up Ottoman miniature
of a mausoleum courtyard. The labels called them *çekül* (plumb bob) and *havayi terazi*, the
"aerial balance", 18th to 19th century.

Anyone who builds needs three things: which way is up, how far, and how high or how far across
when you can't get there. This post follows those three questions from a weight on a string
to satellites. To keep it honest, every plate measures the same piece of ground: a minaret on
its hill, a road down to a quay, and a strait 700 m wide. It's a composite of Istanbul, so the
real Süleymaniye minaret and the Bosphorus narrows are kilometres apart, but the numbers are
the real ones. Each instrument gets a little further across it, until it hits a wall.

## Plumb bob and A-frame: which way is down

Gravity gives you one direction for free. A plumb line shows true vertical, so it can check a
wall. Level is harder, because nothing points sideways for you.

The Egyptian A-frame level borrows a direction from the plumb line. The frame is symmetric, so
the line from its apex to the middle of the crossbar is square to its feet. The surface is
level when the string "bisected the crossbar of the A"
([Britannica](https://britannica.com/technology/A-frame-level)). Tilt the frame by θ and the
string lands $x = h\tan\theta$ from the mark, so a marked crossbar reads slope. The brass
triangles in the case work the same way on a cord stretched between two poles, which carries
level over several metres. Their lineage runs from 11th-century Iraq through Ottoman texts to
1834 ([Jašarević](https://godisnjak.anubih.ba/index.php/godisnjak/article/download/214/212/339)).

It was good enough to build the Great Pyramid, whose pavement falls only about 15 mm from one
corner to the opposite one, across some 325 m
([Cole 1925](https://home.hiwaay.net/~jalison/cole2.html)). The wall: a level tells you how much
higher. It never tells you how far.

## The cubit rod: a length everyone agrees on

"How far" needs a unit, and a stick that carries it. The Egyptian royal cubit was 7 palms or 28
digits, and surviving cubit rods are about 52.5 cm long
([Ancient Egyptian units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egyptian_units_of_measurement)).
Ottoman architects had the *mimar arşını*, which averages 75.774 cm across surviving buildings,
by Aptullah Özdural's survey ([Arshin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arshin_%28length%29)).
A panel in the exhibition cites a miniature of 1579 that shows Sinan holding his at
Süleyman's tomb.

The wall: a rod is as good as its copy, and its error adds up with every length you lay. It
works at the scale of a building, not a province.

## Groma and chorobates: Roman right angles and gradients

Rome had to lay out fields in square grids and run aqueducts for tens of kilometres downhill at
a slope you can barely see. The groma solved the first: a cross on a staff with four plumb
lines, which "allowed projecting right angles and straight lines"
([Groma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groma_%28surveying%29)).

<div class="fx-figure fx-wide">
  <GromaSteps client:visible />
</div>

The strings swing in the wind, and the groma gives directions only. For the gradient, Vitruvius
preferred the chorobates, a bench about twenty feet long with plumb lines and, for windy days,
a groove of water on top, because "dioptrae and levels are deceptive"
([Vitruvius 8.5](https://lexundria.com/vitr/8.5/cf)). The aqueduct to Nîmes falls about 12 m
over 50 km, roughly 25 cm per kilometre
([techno-science](https://www.techno-science.net/fr/definitions/pont-du-gard)). The plate holds
that gradient on a 100 m channel on our hill.

<div class="fx-figure fx-wide">
  <ChorobatesSteps client:visible />
</div>

A single sighting can't see that slope. The Romans got it by levelling again and again; ancient
levelling was typically good to about 25 cm per √km
([Stiros, FIG 2008](https://fig.net/resources/proceedings/fig_proceedings/fig2008/papers/hs02/hs02_03_stiros_2837.pdf)).
The wall: every set-up adds error, and still nothing measures distance quickly.

## Wheels and chains: distance on the ground

Vitruvius also describes a hodometer: a cart wheel four feet across that turns a 400-tooth
drum, which drops a pebble into a bronze bowl every "five thousand feet or one mile"
([Vitruvius 10.9](https://lexundria.com/vitr/10.9/mg)). Count the pebbles at the end of the
road.

<div class="fx-figure">
  <HodometerPlate client:visible />
</div>

A wheel measures the surface, bumps and all. For land, England settled on Edmund Gunter's chain
of 1620: 66 feet in 100 links, with brass tags every ten, and ten square chains to the acre
([Gunter's chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunter%27s_chain)). On a slope it was held level
and the low end dropped with a plumb line.

<div class="fx-figure fx-wide">
  <ChainSteps client:visible />
</div>

Once the land is on a map, you can measure it without walking it. The yellow box in the photo
holds a map measurer: roll its little wheel along a route and the dial counts inches, which
the chart's scale turns into miles. The printed instructions on the box work the example for a
1:250,000 map, "2 1/2 kilometers to the centimeter".

## Triangulation: measure angles instead of distances

The trick is to stop measuring lengths. Chain one baseline carefully, then measure the angles
from both of its ends to a point you can't reach. One side and two angles fix a triangle.

<EqTip note="w is the distance from the baseline to the far point. b is the baseline length. α and β are the angles at its two ends between the baseline and the sight lines. The third angle is 180° minus both, and its sine is sin(α+β).">

$$
w = b\,\frac{\sin\alpha\,\sin\beta}{\sin(\alpha+\beta)}
$$

</EqTip>

The instrument for angles is the theodolite: a telescope that turns on a vertical axis over a
graduated horizontal circle and tips on a horizontal axis against a vertical one. The word first
appears in Leonard Digges's *Pantometria* of 1571, and nobody knows where it comes from
([Theodolite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodolite)). In the plate you drive one.

<div class="fx-figure fx-wide">
  <StraitSteps client:visible />
</div>

The same instrument tipped upward measures a height. Read the elevation of the finial from two
stations in line with the minaret, a known distance apart.

<div class="fx-figure fx-wide">
  <HeightSteps client:visible />
</div>

Each new side becomes the baseline of the next triangle. Willebrord Snellius chained 33 of them
across Holland in 1615 and got his distance about 3.5% too short
([Triangulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangulation_%28surveying%29)). By 1787 Jesse
Ramsden's great theodolite read horizontal angles to within one arcsecond
([Ramsden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramsden_surveying_instruments)). That still matters,
because an angle error grows with distance, and over long sights the Earth itself curves away.

<EqTip note="The miss is how far sideways a sight line ends up at distance d if its angle is off by ε. The drop is how far the Earth's surface falls below a level line after distance d. R is the Earth's radius, about 6,371 km.">

$$
\text{miss} = d\tan\varepsilon \qquad \text{drop} \approx \frac{d^2}{2R}
$$

</EqTip>

<div class="fx-figure">
  <ArcsecondPlate client:visible />
</div>

The Great Trigonometrical Survey of India carried triangles up the subcontinent from a baseline
near Madras laid in 1802 ([GTS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Trigonometrical_Survey)).
In 1856 it announced that Peak XV was 29,002 ft, measured from stations more than 100 miles
away, after correcting for curvature, refraction and the pull of the mountains on the plumb line
([GPS World](https://www.gpsworld.com/peak-xv-the-framework-that-measured-mount-everest/)). The
2020 Nepal-China figure is 8,848.86 m, or 29,032 ft
([AP](https://www.clickondetroit.com/news/world/2020/12/08/china-nepal-say-everest-a-bit-higher-than-past-measurements/)).
Brass circles and log tables were off by about 0.1%.

The theodolite in the exhibition still sits in its wooden box. Its label is from J. Verdoux,
"Instruments de précision", at Place du Tunnel and the Grande Rue de Péra. Tünel, the
funicular, opened in 1875
([lepetitjournal](https://lepetitjournal.com/istanbul/le-tunel-distanbul-ou-le-plus-petit-metro-du-monde-340929)),
so the label is from after that: a shop at the top of today's İstiklal Caddesi.

The wall: every length in a network of triangles is scaled from one measured baseline. Get it
wrong and the whole map is wrong by the same fraction.

## Baseline bars: a ruler that ignores the weather

Steel grows about 11.5 millionths of its length per degree, so a thermometer on the day matters
as much as the bars. And a thermometer reads the air, not the bar. For the Irish survey,
Thomas Colby had Thomas Drummond build bars of two metals, iron and brass, joined at the centre
with "steel tongues fixed across the ends"
([Our Irish Heritage](https://www.ouririshheritage.org/?p=31608)). As the bars grow, the tongues
tilt, and one point on each tongue stays still. Herschel describes the result: two fine dots
that "remain exactly at the same distance"
([Herschel 1867](https://geology.19thcenturyscience.org/books/1867-Herschel-Lectures/PDFpages/185.pdf)).

<div class="fx-figure fx-wide">
  <BarSteps client:visible />
</div>

The Lough Foyle base, measured with them in 1827-28, was about eight miles long, and its 1960
remeasurement came out about one inch longer
([Geograph](https://www.geograph.org.uk/snippet/1448)). It also took some sixty days of
measuring by seventy men ([Our Irish Heritage](https://www.ouririshheritage.org/?p=31608)).
The wall: superb, and far too slow.

## Light: the total station

In the 1940s Erik Bergstrand of the Swedish Geographical Survey sent flickering light to a
mirror and back and compared the phase of the flicker going out with the flicker coming back.
On a 6 km base in 1947 he turned it around and measured the speed of light to
299,793.1 ± 0.2 km/s; the commercial Geodimeter that followed measured distances to 1 part in
300,000 ([Smithsonian](https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_748815)).

<EqTip note="D is the distance. λ/2 is half the wavelength of the flicker, the length of one 'tape' out and back. N is the number of whole tapes, fixed with a second, coarser flicker. Δφ is the phase difference between the light going out and coming back, as a fraction of a full cycle.">

$$
D = \left(N + \frac{\Delta\varphi}{360^\circ}\right)\frac{\lambda}{2}
$$

</EqTip>

<div class="fx-figure fx-wide">
  <EdmSteps client:visible />
</div>

A total station is that rangefinder built into a theodolite with a computer. A Leica TS13 reads
angles to 1″ in its best version, the same as Ramsden's giant, and distances to a prism to 1 mm
plus 1 mm per kilometre
([Leica](https://cm.leica-geosystems.com/-/media/files/leicageosystems/products/datasheets/leica-ts13-lr-ds.ashx)).
The wall: it still needs to see the other end.

## GPS and lidar: positions without a line of sight

Satellites remove the line of sight. A base receiver on a known point and a rover on the point
you want share the same satellite signals and cancel most of their errors. This is RTK, and a
survey receiver promises 8 mm plus 1 ppm horizontally and 15 mm plus 1 ppm vertically, within 30
km of the base
([Trimble](https://spaces.awi.de/download/attachments/183566393/Trimble_SPS855_specification.pdf?api=v2)).
It uses the same carrier-phase idea as the Geodimeter, only with the satellites as the far end
([Penn State](https://e-education.psu.edu/geog862/node/1749)).

<div class="fx-figure">
  <GpsPlate client:visible />
</div>

Airborne lidar does the same for a whole landscape: a plane sweeps the ground with laser
pulses, at least 8 points per square metre with 10 cm vertical accuracy in the USGS's QL1
standard ([USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/3d-elevation-program/topographic-data-quality-levels-qls)).
The minaret, the road and both quays become a cloud of points in an afternoon.

Put all of these on one scale, the error per kilometre of length, and the climb is not a
straight one. The pyramid builders levelled better than Snellius triangulated, four thousand
years later.

## The end of the series

That closes the series: [time, latitude, the astrolabe and the ground](/articles/instruments).
Every post had the same shape: an instrument that worked, a wall it hit, a better instrument.
They also end in the same place. GPS is a clock problem, an angle problem and a surveying
problem at once: satellites carrying atomic clocks, located like stars, used to find where you
stand on the Earth to a centimetre.
