A weight on a string may be the oldest measuring instrument still sold in hardware shops. This post
climbs from it to the satellites that now tell a surveyor where she stands to the centimetre.
Every rung exists because the one below it hit a wall.

## Finding level with a plumb line

Gravity gives you one free direction: down. A plumb line shows it, so it tells you whether a
wall is vertical. Level is harder, because nothing points sideways for you.

The Egyptian answer was the A-frame level: two legs, a crossbar, a plumb line from the apex. The
surface is level when the line "bisected the crossbar of the A"
([Britannica](https://britannica.com/technology/A-frame-level)). The frame is symmetric, so the
line from the apex to the centre of the crossbar is square to the feet. The string agrees with it
only when the feet are level. Tilt the frame by θ and the string lands $x = h\tan\theta$ from the
centre mark, where $h$ is the drop from apex to crossbar. Mark the crossbar and the same tool
reads slope.

<div class="fx-figure">
  <LevelPlate client:visible />
</div>

It was good enough. The pavement of the Great Pyramid slopes by about 15 mm from one corner to
the opposite one, across some 325 m
([Cole 1925](https://home.hiwaay.net/~jalison/cole2.html)). Europeans still used A-frames
"until the mid-19th century"
([Britannica](https://britannica.com/technology/A-frame-level)).

The brass triangles in the case are the same idea in a better package. Hang one by its two
hooks from a cord stretched between two poles, slide the far end of the cord until the bob sits
on the mark, and the cord is level over many metres. Read the poles, move forward, repeat. The
lineage runs from 11th-century Iraq through Ottoman texts to 1834
([Jašarević](https://godisnjak.anubih.ba/index.php/godisnjak/article/download/214/212/339)), and
the Pera Museum's teacher booklet says Sinan used one to build the Kırkçeşme water line
([Pera Museum](https://www.peramuzesi.org.tr/Images/pdf/ogretmen-kitapcik/aavok-copy.pdf)).

The wall: a level tells you that two points are at the same height. It does not tell you how far
apart they are.

## Units of length: the cubit and the arşın

For that you need a unit, and a stick that carries it. The Egyptian royal cubit was 7 palms or
28 digits, and surviving cubit rods are about 52.5 cm long
([Ancient Egyptian units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egyptian_units_of_measurement)).

Ottoman architects had their own: the *mimar arşını*, the architect's cubit, which averages
75.774 cm across surviving buildings, by Aptullah Özdural's survey
([Arshin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arshin_%28length%29)). That is about 1.44 Egyptian
cubits. The panel beside the levels cites a miniature of 1579 (Chester Beatty Library, T 413)
that shows Sinan at Süleyman's tomb, holding his.

The panel quotes Sinan's memoirs. In the exhibition's translation he says he "worked resolute
like the fixed arm of a compass" and "observed the center and the periphery" (Sâî Mustafa
Çelebi, *Tezkiretü'l-Ebniye*, as translated on the panel). A builder describing himself as a
drawing instrument is my favourite thing in the whole exhibition.

The wall: rods and levels work at the scale of a building. Carry them across 50 km of hills
and the small errors add up.

## Roman levelling over 50 km

Roman surveyors had the groma (a cross on a pole with four plumb lines, for straight lines and
right angles) and the chorobates, a 20-foot bench with plumb lines and a water channel cut into
its top for windy days. Vitruvius preferred it, because "dioptrae and levels are deceptive"
([Vitruvius 8.5.1](https://lexundria.com/vitr/8.5/cf)).

The aqueduct that crosses the Pont du Gard to Nîmes falls about 12 m over 50 km, roughly 25 cm
per kilometre
([techno-science](https://www.techno-science.net/fr/definitions/pont-du-gard)). That slope is
an angle of under one arcminute, held on average over the whole route. Estimates of the total
drop range from about 11 to 13 m.

Earlier, on Samos, Eupalinos had a tunnel dug from both ends through a mountain. The two
crews met with an offset of about 60 cm over a kilometre
([Stiros, FIG 2008](https://fig.net/resources/proceedings/fig_proceedings/fig2008/papers/hs02/hs02_03_stiros_2837.pdf)).

The wall: all of this is step by step. Every leapfrog of the poles adds a little error, and you
cannot pace across a river or up a minaret.

## Triangulation: distances from angles

The trick is to stop measuring lengths. Measure one baseline carefully, then measure the angles
from both ends to a far point. A triangle with one known side and two known angles is fixed.

<EqTip note="d_A is the distance from end A of the baseline to the target. b is the baseline length. α and β are the angles at A and B between the baseline and the sight lines. The third angle of the triangle is 180° minus both, and its sine is sin(α+β).">

$$
d_A = b\,\frac{\sin\beta}{\sin(\alpha+\beta)}
$$

</EqTip>

Each new side becomes the baseline of the next triangle. Willebrord Snellius chained 33 of them
across Holland in 1615 and got his distance about 3.5% too short
([Triangulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangulation_%28surveying%29)). The Cassinis
covered France, and their map came out in 181 sheets at 1:86,400
([Cartography of France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartography_of_France)).

The same idea measures a height. Stand at two points in line with a minaret, a baseline $b$
apart, and read the elevation angle at each.

<EqTip note="h is the height. b is the baseline walked toward the minaret. α is the elevation angle from the far station, β from the near one. β minus α is the angle at the top of the minaret between the two sight lines. When it is small, the bottom line is tiny and any error in the angles is magnified.">

$$
h = \frac{b\,\sin\alpha\,\sin\beta}{\sin(\beta-\alpha)}
$$

</EqTip>

  <TriangulationPlate client:visible />
</div>

The error is lopsided. With the stations 200 m and 275 m from the minaret and each angle off by
half a degree, the answer lands anywhere from 63.5 m to 94.1 m: 12.5 m too low at worst, but
18.1 m too high. Near the top the two sight lines are almost parallel, so a small twist slides
their crossing a long way up.

Snellius's 3.5% is far worse than the pyramid builders' level, so this ladder does not climb
evenly. Levelling with plumb lines and water was superb early. Long distances lagged until the
angles got good.

## The theodolite: an instrument for measuring angles

Getting the angles good is the theodolite's job. The word first appears in Leonard Digges's
*Pantometria* of 1571, and nobody knows where it comes from
([Theodolite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodolite)). The idea is two graduated circles on
two axes: turn the telescope around the vertical axis to read a horizontal angle, tip it about
the horizontal axis to read a vertical one.

The one in the exhibition still sits in its wooden box. The label inside the door is from J.
Verdoux, "Instruments de précision", successor of L. Poirier, at Place du Tunnel and the Grande
Rue de Péra. Tünel, the funicular, opened in 1875
([lepetitjournal](https://lepetitjournal.com/istanbul/le-tunel-distanbul-ou-le-plus-petit-metro-du-monde-340929)),
so the label is from after that. The shop stood at the Tünel end of today's İstiklal Caddesi.

The high point of hand-made circles was Jesse Ramsden's great theodolite of about 1787: a 36-inch
circle, horizontal angles to within one arcsecond
([Ramsden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramsden_surveying_instruments)). One arcsecond is a 2.4 cm coin seen from 5 km away. It still matters, because the error grows with
distance, and over long sights the Earth itself curves away.

<EqTip note="The miss is how far sideways a sight line ends up at distance d if its angle is off by ε. The drop is how far the Earth's surface falls below a level line after distance d. R is the Earth's radius, about 6,371 km.">

$$
\text{miss} = d\tan\varepsilon \qquad \text{drop} \approx \frac{d^2}{2R}
$$

</EqTip>

<div class="fx-figure">
  <ArcsecondPlate client:visible />
</div>

That is the problem the Great Trigonometrical Survey of India took on. It began on 10 April 1802
with a baseline of about 12 km near Madras
([GTS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Trigonometrical_Survey)). Its big theodolite
reportedly weighed about half a tonne and took twelve men to carry
([GPS World](https://www.gpsworld.com/peak-xv-the-framework-that-measured-mount-everest/)).
In 1856 the survey announced that Peak XV was 29,002 ft, measured from stations more than 100
miles away, after correcting for curvature, refraction and the pull of the mountains on the
plumb line (same source). The 2020 Nepal-China figure is 8,848.86 m, or 29,032 ft
([AP](https://www.clickondetroit.com/news/world/2020/12/08/china-nepal-say-everest-a-bit-higher-than-past-measurements/)).
A team with brass circles and log tables was off by about 30 ft, or 0.1%.

## Scale drawings: taking measurements back to the building site

Measuring is half the story. The other half is carrying a measurement from paper to stone. The
second photo above is a drawing of a mosque door by Mehmed Nuri, a student at the
Mekteb-i Sanayi-i Osmani, the Ottoman vocational school founded in Istanbul in 1868-69
([TDV](https://islamansiklopedisi.org.tr/islahhane)). The label dates it 16 May 1326 in the Rumi
calendar, which is 29 May 1910
([Rumi calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rumi_calendar)).

The door is drawn reduced. The joinery sections beside it are much larger: as far as I can read
the Ottoman, they are marked *mikyas ½*, half full size, because a carpenter needs the profile
itself, not a picture of it. A scale drawing is a theodolite run backwards: the measurements go
from the paper out into the world.

## Total stations, GPS and lidar

A modern total station is a theodolite with a laser rangefinder and a computer. A Leica TS13
reads angles to 1″ in its best version, the same as Ramsden's giant, and distances to a prism
to 1 mm plus 1 mm per kilometre
([Leica](https://cm.leica-geosystems.com/-/media/files/leicageosystems/products/datasheets/leica-ts13-lr-ds.ashx)).

Then the baseline moved into orbit. RTK satellite receivers give a position to about 8 mm plus
1 ppm horizontally, within 30 km of a base station
([Trimble](https://spaces.awi.de/download/attachments/183566393/Trimble_SPS855_specification.pdf?api=v2)).
No line of sight, no triangle chain. And airborne LiDAR sweeps the ground with laser pulses,
at least 8 points per square metre with 10 cm vertical accuracy in the USGS's QL1 standard
([USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/3d-elevation-program/topographic-data-quality-levels-qls)).

Put all of these on one scale, the error in a measured length per kilometre, and the
climb is not a straight one.

## Where the three series meet

That closes the series. Three ladders, for [time, the sky and the ground](/articles/instruments),
and the same shape every time: a crude method that worked, a wall, a better instrument. They
also meet at the top. GPS is a clock problem, an angle problem and a surveying problem at once:
satellites carrying atomic clocks, read like stars, used to find where you stand on the Earth.
