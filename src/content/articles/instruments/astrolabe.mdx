The case in Istanbul with the two wooden quadrants also had a small table of brass: two pocket
compasses, a quadrant on a stand with two plumb lines, and the corner of a *kıblenüma*, a dial for
finding the direction of Mecca. These were a timekeeper's tools. An article on the Ottoman
*muvakkithane*, the timekeeper's room beside a mosque, lists the tools muvakkits used: "rub’u
tahtası, usturlab, kıblenüma gibi aletleri" ([Zorlu, Belleten 2014](https://belleten.gov.tr/eng/full-text/215/tur)).

There was no usturlab in that case, so this post is about it.

## The word: star-taker

*Astrolabe* comes from the Greek *astrolabos*, "star-taking", from *astron* (star) and
*lambanein* (to take) ([Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/astrolabe)). Arabic borrowed
it as *asṭurlāb* or *usṭurlāb*, and also translated it: *ākhidh al-nujūm*, "taker of the stars"
([King 1981, via Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrolabe)). Persian and Ottoman
Turkish kept the Arabic spelling, اسطرلاب. Mustafa ibn Ali, timekeeper of the Selim I mosque,
called his 1544 handbook *Risālat al-asṭurlāb al-Selīmī*
([BEA](https://islamsci.mcgill.ca/RASI/BEA/Ali_al-Muwaqqit_BEA.htm)). Modern Turkish says
*usturlap*, with the final b hardened to p as Turkish does at the end of a word. Medieval Latin made it
*astrolabium*, Old French *astrelabe* (around 1160), and English had it by the mid-14th century. In
1391 Chaucer wrote a treatise on it for his son: "Lyte Lowys my sone"
([Fordham](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/halsall/source/chaucer-astro.asp)).

There is also a story that the word is Arabic. Read *asṭur* as "lines" and *Lāb* as a son of the
prophet Idrīs, who invented the thing, and you get "the lines of Lāb". The 10th-century
scholar al-Qummī mentioned it and al-Khwārizmī rejected it ([King 1981, via
Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrolabe)). It still travelled: a French astrologer
in the 1490s wrote that astrolabe means "comme les lignes de Lab"
([DFSM](https://dfsm.elan-numerique.fr/entry/pdf/4156)). It loses because the Greek word and the
Greek treatises on the instrument are centuries older than any Arabic one, and the Arabic
*ākhidh al-nujūm* is a translation of that Greek word. (Hamza al-Isfahani tried a Persian origin
too, and al-Bīrūnī criticised him for it.)

## The problem: the sky turns

A timekeeper needs the hour, and the only clock is the sky. The sun rises in the east, climbs,
crosses the meridian at noon and sets, on a path that changes every day of the year. So does
every star. Measuring the sun's height is easy. Turning that height into an hour is spherical
trigonometry, the relation between your latitude φ, the sun's declination δ (how far north or
south of the celestial equator it is today), its altitude h and its hour angle H:

<EqTip note="h: the sun's altitude above the horizon. φ: your latitude (41° in Istanbul). δ: the sun's declination for the date. H: its hour angle, the turn of the sky since noon, 15° per hour.">

$$
\sin h = \sin\varphi\,\sin\delta + \cos\varphi\,\cos\delta\,\cos H
$$

</EqTip>

Doing that by hand, every few hours, every day, with tables of sines, is slow. The first answer was
to build a model of the sky and turn it.

## A model of the sky: the armillary sphere

An armillary sphere is the sky made of rings: a horizon and a meridian that stay fixed, and inside
them a sphere with the equator, the ecliptic (the sun's yearly path) and a polar axis. It is the
old instrument; Hipparchus probably used one, and Ptolemy's *Almagest* describes one
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrolabe)). Tilt the axis to your latitude, put the
sun on the ecliptic for the date, and turn the sphere until the sun is as high as you measured.
The equator ring then tells you the hour.

It works, but it is the sky in three dimensions: big, bulky, every circle a separate ring, and the
rings hide each other. Could the sky be flat?

## Flattening the sky: stereographic projection

There is a way to draw a sphere on a plane that keeps every circle a circle. Put your eye at the
south celestial pole and project each point of the sky onto the plane of the equator. A star at
declination δ lands at a distance

<EqTip note="r: distance from the centre of the plate (the north celestial pole), in units of the equator's radius. δ: the star's declination. A star on the equator lands at r = 1; the north pole at the centre.">

$$
r = \tan\!\left(\frac{90^\circ - \delta}{2}\right)
$$

</EqTip>

from the centre, and as the sky turns it turns round the centre. Circles of equal altitude over a
given place also land as circles, just off-centre. The geometry is Greek: Hipparchus probably knew
it ([Pingree, Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/?p=6120)). The earliest
certain instrument is late antique: Theon of Alexandria wrote on it in the 4th century, and John
Philoponus's treatise from about 550 is the oldest that survives
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrolabe)).

The catch is in the last step. The stars' circles are the same for everyone; the horizon and the
height circles depend on where you stand. One drawing cannot hold both, unless they can move
against each other.

## The astrolabe: a plate for the place, a spider for the sky

That is the planispheric astrolabe. The height circles go on a fixed plate cut for one latitude.
The sky goes on a pierced disc laid over it, the rete, which the Arabic texts call the spider
(*ʿankabūt*, Turkish *örümcek*) and Chaucer calls the "riet ... shapen in manere of a net". Turn
the rete and the sky turns over your horizon. Al-Fazārī, in the 8th century, "was said to have
been the first Muslim to construct a plane astrolabe"
([Plofker, BEA](https://islamsci.mcgill.ca/RASI/BEA/Fazari_BEA.htm)), and the oldest dated one
that survives was made by Nasṭūlus in 927/28
([BEA](https://islamsci.mcgill.ca/RASI/BEA/Nastulus_BEA.pdf)).

## Telling the time with it

Now use it. You need one measurement, the sun's altitude, taken with the alidade on the back, and
the date. The rest is turning and reading. Al-Ṣūfī wrote a treatise on the astrolabe with over a
thousand uses ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd_al-Rahman_al-Sufi)); this is the one
a *muvakkit* did every day.

<div class="fx-figure fx-wide">
  <TellTimePlate client:visible />
</div>

The same moves answer other questions. Put the sun's point on the eastern horizon and the rule
gives sunrise; at 41° N the day runs from about 9 hours in December to 15 in June. Sight a star
at night instead of the sun and you get the time in the dark. A shadow square on the back turns
the alidade into a height-measurer for minarets, and many astrolabes carry gazetteers for the
direction of Mecca ([MHS Oxford](https://www.mhs.ox.ac.uk/astrolabe/catalogue/Glossary/glossary.html)).
The sailor's "mariner's astrolabe" is a different object that kept only the degree ring and the
alidade, for latitude at sea (it is in the [latitude post](/articles/instruments/sky)).

## The plate only works where it was cut

The plate in the plate above is for 41° N. Carry it to Cairo and the sun's measured height lands
on the wrong circle. Makers' answer was more plates: an Ottoman astrolabe by ʿAbdī, dated 1713/4,
has four, for 21° to 42° ([MHS Oxford](https://www.mhs.ox.ac.uk/astrolabe/catalogue/browseReport/Astrolabe_ID=132.html)).
The cleverer answer came from al-Andalus. In the 11th century al-Zarqālī projected the sky onto the
meridian plane instead, where every horizon is a straight line through the centre, so one plate
serves every latitude: the *saphea Azarchelis*
([MHS Oxford](https://www.mhs.ox.ac.uk/astrolabe/catalogue/Glossary/mainGlossary/Glossary_ID=55.html)).

</div>

Istanbul had both kinds. Taqī al-Dīn's observatory, finished in 1577 and destroyed in 1580,
listed the astrolabe and the universal astrolabe among its instruments
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople_observatory_of_Taqi_ad-Din)).

## Half an astrolabe on a board: the quadrants

Universal or not, an astrolabe was brass, expensive, and coarse at hand size. The Met's note on one
made in Mashhad in 1654/55: "not precise enough for astronomical observations ... but served well for telling
time" ([Met](https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/works-of-art/63.166a-j)). A muvakkit needed only one
city and the daytime sun. Fold the plate in half (the morning half mirrors the afternoon), fold it
again, replace the rete with a thread and a bead, and you have the almucantar quadrant, "an
astrolabe folded back twice on itself" ([Museo Galileo](https://catalogue.museogalileo.it/multimedia/QuadrantBis.html)).

<div class="fx-figure fx-wide">
  <QuadrantPlate client:visible />
</div>

The sine quadrant went one step further and replaced the drawn circles with a 60 by 60 grid that
computes the same equation; it stayed in use into the 19th century
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrant_(instrument))). Mustafa ibn Ali shows the
shift: his astrolabe treatise is his longest work, but his quadrant handbook of 1529 is the one
that survives in about 100 copies
([BEA](https://islamsci.mcgill.ca/RASI/BEA/Ali_al-Muwaqqit_BEA.htm)). The two boards in the photo
are of that kind, and the [time post](/articles/instruments/time) works one step by step.

## The astrolabe goes into the clock

The last turn of the story is that a machine took over the turning. Abī Bakr of Isfahan built a
geared astrolabe in 1235, and Richard of Wallingford put a rotating star map in his clock around
1330 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrolabe)). When Prague's astronomical clock
entered the record on 9 October 1410, its astrolabe dial "comprised the entire clock"
([Linda Hall Library](https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/prague-astronomical-clock/)).
The gearing has to know one thing the reader of a hand astrolabe never notices: the stars turn
once in 23 h 56 min, the sun once in 24 h.

Once clocks kept equal hours, the muvakkit's job slowly became setting and repairing them. The
