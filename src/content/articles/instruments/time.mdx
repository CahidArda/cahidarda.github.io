The clock in the photo below hangs in a glass case in Istanbul. The label says it was made in
the first half of the 20th century by the clockmaker Mustafa Şem'i, and the dial is signed
"Mustafa ve Nâsır ve Şürekâsı", Istanbul, number 31. A brass dial, a long wooden rod, a brass
bob the size of a dinner plate. It is not old by museum standards. What caught me was the
shelf next to it: two wooden boards for telling the time from the sun, one from 1753 and one
from 1860.

So in one case you had two ways of answering "what time is it?", separated by a century or two
and about a metre of glass. This post is the ladder between them, and the rungs above and below.

## A stick and its shadow

The oldest sundial we have is Egyptian, from the reign of Thutmose III around 1500 BCE: an
L-shaped stone with hour marks, turned round at noon to face the other way
([Oxford, Cabinet](https://www.cabinet.ox.ac.uk/node/9784)).

A shadow gives you the time of day, but only in the sense the ancient world meant it. The day
from sunrise to sunset was split into twelve hours, whatever its length
([Museo Galileo](https://catalogue.museogalileo.it/indepth/HourLines.html)). In Istanbul, by my
arithmetic, a daytime hour stretches from about 45 minutes in December to 75 in June.

The wall: no sun, no time. Clouds and nights are a problem for a shadow.

## Water that keeps falling

A water clock works in the dark. The oldest complete one, from the reign of Amenhotep III
(c. 1390 to 1350 BCE), is a stone bowl with a hole near the bottom and twelve columns of marks
inside, one per month, because night hours changed length through the year
([McMaster, Ancient Egypt Archive](https://aea.mcmaster.ca/index.php/en/database/water-clocks/wco-1-amenhotep-iii-karnak)).

Its flaw is physics. As the bowl empties, the pressure drops and the flow slows. Around 270 BCE
Ctesibius of Alexandria fixed that with a header tank kept at constant level, feeding a vessel
whose float lifts a pointer ([Oxford, Cabinet](https://www.cabinet.ox.ac.uk/node/9787)). In 1206
al-Jazari's *Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices* turned the idea into theatre: in
his elephant clock "every half hour, the bird on the dome whistled"
([The Met](https://www.metmuseum.org/en/art/collection/search/451402)).

The wall: water changes with temperature, holes clog, and the clock still has to be set from
the sky. Nobody has a good figure for how well these kept time; a quarter of an hour a day is
the usual order of magnitude.

## Reading the hour off the sun

Which brings us to the boards. A sine quadrant (*rubʿ al-mujayyab*, in Turkish *rubu tahtası*)
is a quarter circle with a 60 by 60 grid on its face and a thread hanging from the corner. It
was described by al-Khwārizmī in 9th-century Baghdad and was used mostly for prayer times
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine_quadrant)). The person who used it was the
*muvakkit*, the mosque timekeeper. The first Ottoman *muvakkithane*, a timekeeper's room next to
a mosque, is usually taken to be the one at the Fatih Mosque, around 1470
([Belleten](https://belleten.gov.tr/eng/full-text/215/tur)).

You sight the sun along the top edge and the thread falls to the sun's altitude, $h$. Follow the
grid line from that point to the edge and you read $60 \sin h$. The board is a sine table you
can hold. With the date and your latitude, the altitude gives the hour angle $H$, the sun's
distance from noon:

<EqTip note="h is the sun's altitude, φ your latitude (41° for Istanbul), δ the sun's declination (how far north or south of the equator it is that day, from −23.4° to +23.4°), and H the hour angle: how far the sun has turned past noon, 15° per hour. The muvakkit solved it for H on the board, step by step.">

$$
\sin h = \sin\varphi \sin\delta + \cos\varphi \cos\delta \cos H
$$

</EqTip>

Every term is a sine or a cosine, and the grid does products and quotients with similar
triangles. Drag the thread:

The $\sin\varphi$ and $\cos\varphi$ never change if you never move, which is why the labels on
both boards say they were made for the 41st parallel, "on which Istanbul also lies". Next to
them hangs a study sheet from 1848: a quadrant drawn in ink with one red line across the grid.
Someone wrote on it, in Ottoman, roughly "quadrant drawing, but faulty" (museum label).

The wall: the quadrant gives a reading, not a running clock. Between readings you still need
something that ticks.

## Gears, and a stutter

Mechanical clocks appear in Europe in the late 13th century. Their heart was the verge and
foliot: a crown-shaped wheel pushed by a weight, and a bar with two weights that swings one way,
then the other, letting the wheel advance a tooth at a time. The first ones drifted by an hour
or two a day; the best, by the mid-1600s, about 15 minutes
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verge_escapement)).

In 1559 (some sources say 1556) Taqi al-Din wrote the first Ottoman book on mechanical clocks,
and in his Istanbul observatory he used one for timing the stars: "We divided each minute into
five seconds" ([Muslim Heritage](https://muslimheritage.com/instruments-istanbul-observatory/)).

The wall: a foliot has no rhythm of its own. It swings as fast as the weight pushes it, so
anything that changes the push changes the time.

## The pendulum

A pendulum does have a rhythm of its own. Galileo put isochronism (the swing takes the same time
whatever its size, if the swing is small) in writing in 1602
([Museo Galileo](https://museogalileo.it/en/galileo/life.html)). Christiaan Huygens built the
first pendulum clock in 1656, and its error was "less than 1 minute a day"
([NIST](https://www.nist.gov/node/446006)). The period depends on the length, and almost on
nothing else:

<EqTip note="T is the time for one full swing, there and back. L is the length from the pivot to the bob's centre, and g is gravity's acceleration, about 9.81 m/s². Four times the length doubles the period. The weight of the bob does not appear at all.">

$$
T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}
$$

</EqTip>

Around 1670 English clockmakers added the anchor escapement, which cut the swing from about 100°
to 4 to 6° ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anchor_escapement)). A small swing keeps
the formula honest. Here is that movement, with a weight driving the wheel and a pendulum you can
lengthen:

At 0.994 m the period is exactly 2 seconds. Each half swing releases half a tooth, and a 30-tooth
wheel turns once a minute, so it can carry the seconds hand
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anchor_escapement)). The same sensitivity is the
weakness: if summer heat lengthens that rod by one millimetre, the clock loses about 43 seconds a
day.

Mustafa Şem'i's clock is a weight-driven pendulum clock of this family, but in Istanbul the same
hands could tell a different time. Ottoman time counted the day from sunset, so sunset was 12 o'clock and the clocks had to be reset to it
every evening ([Bir, Acar and Kaçar](https://www.nazariyat.org/content/5-sayilar/2-2/3-m0007/atilla-bir-sinasi-acar-mustafa-kacar.pdf)).
The label does not say which way No. 31 was set, so try both:

The poet Ahmet Haşim missed the old way. In his essay "Müslüman Saati" he wrote of a twelve-hour
day "ziyada başlayıp ziyada biten", beginning and ending in light
([Milli Gazete](https://www.milligazete.com.tr/ahmet-hasimden-musluman-saati)). Law No. 697
moved Turkey to a day starting at midnight, in force from 2 January 1926
([Lexpera](https://www.lexpera.com.tr/mevzuat/kanunlar/gunun-yirmi-dort-saate-taksimine-dair-kanun-697)).

The wall: a pendulum needs a floor that stays still. Put it on a rolling ship and it stops being
a clock.

## A clock that goes to sea

At sea, time is longitude. The Earth "turns through one degree of longitude every four minutes"
([Royal Museums Greenwich](https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/time/what-longitude)), so a ship that
carries home-port time and compares it with local noon knows how far east or west it is. Britain's
Longitude Act of 1714 offered £20,000 for half a degree, two minutes of time after a long voyage
([xnatmap](https://xnatmap.org/adnm/docs/2013/long/reward/longreward.htm)).

John Harrison's H4, a large pocket watch, sailed to Jamaica in 1761. After 81 days at sea
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Harrison)), with its known rate allowed for, "it
was only 5 seconds slow" ([Linda Hall Library](https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/john-harrison)).

The wall: a balance wheel and a spring are still metal, and metal creeps with heat and age.

## Crystals and atoms

In 1927 Warren Marrison and J. W. Horton at Bell Labs built a clock around a vibrating quartz
crystal ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warren_Marrison)). In 1969 Seiko put one on
a wrist, the Astron, good to five seconds a month
([IEEE Milestone](https://ethw.org/Milestones:Electronic_Quartz_Wristwatch,_1969)).

In 1955 Louis Essen and Jack Parry at NPL built "the first successful atomic clock", accurate to
a second in 300 years ([Science Museum Group](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co855/caesium-atomic-clock-1955)).
Since 1967 the second has been defined by caesium: 9,192,631,770 periods of a particular
microwave transition ([BIPM](https://www.bipm.org/committees/cg/cgpm/13-1967/resolution-1)).
Optical clocks now go further. JILA's 2015 strontium clock "would neither gain nor lose one
second in some 15 billion years" ([NIST](https://www.nist.gov/node/392596)), and in 2025 NIST
reported an aluminium ion clock better still
([optics.org](https://optics.org/news/nists-ion-clock-claims-record-for-most-accurate-in-the-world)).

The wall, for once, is on our side: very little needs time this good. One thing that does is in
your pocket.

## Time is position

Your phone finds itself by timing radio signals from satellites. Light travels about 30 cm in a
nanosecond, so the distance to a satellite is a clock reading in disguise:

<EqTip note="d is the distance to the satellite, c the speed of light (299,792,458 m/s, exact by definition), and Δt how long the signal took. With four satellites the receiver also solves for its own clock's error.">

$$
d = c\,\Delta t
$$

</EqTip>

Harrison and GPS are the same trick at different speeds:

Up there, relativity gets a vote. The satellites feel weaker gravity and move fast, and the net
effect is that "a GPS satellite clock will gain about 38 microseconds per day"
([GPS World](https://www.gpsworld.com/inside-the-box-gps-and-relativity/)). Left alone, that
grows into range errors of up to about 11 km between daily corrections. So the clocks are set to
tick slow on the ground, at 10.22999999543 MHz instead of 10.23 MHz, and run right in orbit.

## The other half

Notice what the longitude trick needed. A clock that keeps home time, yes, but also a measurement
of local noon: the sun's highest point, read off the sky with an angle. Harrison solved one half.
The other half is an instrument that measures angles on a rolling deck, and that is the
[next post](/articles/instruments/sky).
