made in the first half of the 20th century by the clockmaker Mustafa Şem'i. On the shelf next to
it sit two wooden boards for telling the time from the sun, one from 1753 and one from 1860. Two
answers to "what time is it?", a metre of glass apart.

The question underneath both is the same: how far has the sun turned since noon? Every device in
this post is a better way of carrying that answer, through clouds, through the night, across a
week, across an ocean, down to a nanosecond. Each one exists because the one before it failed at
something specific, so each plate below ends where its device breaks.

The right half of every plate is the same picture: the sun over Istanbul at the true time, what
the device says, and a gauge of how many seconds a day it gets wrong. Watch the red mark on the
gauge move right.

## The sundial: the hour from a shadow

The oldest sundial we have is Egyptian, from the reign of Thutmose III around 1500 BCE: an
L-shaped stone with hour marks, turned round at noon
([Oxford, Cabinet](https://www.cabinet.ox.ac.uk/node/9784)). Its hours were not ours. The
daylight was cut into twelve, whatever its length
([Museo Galileo](https://catalogue.museogalileo.it/indepth/HourLines.html)), so a summer hour was
longer than a winter one.

<div class="fx-figure fx-wide">
  <SundialPlate client:visible />
</div>

In Istanbul, by my arithmetic, that hour runs from about 45 minutes in December to 75 in June.
Tilting the shadow edge to point at the pole fixes the hours, and the same lines then work all
year. Nothing fixes the night.

## The water clock: time in the dark

A water clock keeps going when the sun is gone. The oldest complete one, from the reign of
Amenhotep III (c. 1390 to 1350 BCE), is a stone bowl, 46 cm across at the top and 26 cm at the
bottom, with a hole near its base and twelve columns of marks inside, one per month
([McMaster, Ancient Egypt Archive](https://aea.mcmaster.ca/index.php/en/database/water-clocks/wco-1-amenhotep-iii-karnak)).

<div class="fx-figure fx-wide">
  <WaterClockPlate client:visible />
</div>

The physics is against an even scale. Water leaves a hole faster when there is more of it above
the hole, so a full bowl drains quickly and a nearly empty one barely drips. The Karnak bowl is
wider at the top than at the bottom, and sloping sides like these let the water drip "at a nearly
constant rate" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_clock)). Ctesibius of Alexandria, around 270 BCE,
solved it properly with a header tank kept at a constant level, so the flow into a second vessel
never changed and a float rose at a steady rate
([Oxford, Cabinet](https://www.cabinet.ox.ac.uk/node/9787)).

But grit, cold water and a slightly wrong start all carry through the night, and the start itself
has to come from the sun. Setting a clock needs the time from the sun to the minute, at any hour,
and a shadow on a dial is too coarse for that.

## The sine quadrant: the hour from the sun's height

That is the job of the two boards in the case. A sine quadrant (*rubʿ al-mujayyab*, in Turkish
*rubu tahtası*) is a quarter circle with a 60 by 60 grid and a thread hanging from the corner.
al-Khwārizmī described one in 9th-century Baghdad
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine_quadrant)). In Ottoman cities it belonged to the
*muvakkit*, the mosque timekeeper; the first Ottoman timekeeper's room is usually taken to be the
one at the Fatih Mosque, around 1470 ([Belleten](https://belleten.gov.tr/eng/full-text/215/tur)).

You sight the sun along the top edge and the thread falls to its altitude $h$. With the date and
your latitude, that altitude gives the hour angle $H$, the sun's distance from noon:

<EqTip note="h is the sun's altitude, φ your latitude (41° for Istanbul), δ the sun's declination that day (how far north or south of the equator it stands, −23.4° to +23.4°), and H the hour angle: how far the sun has turned from noon, 15° per hour.">

$$
\sin h = \sin\varphi \sin\delta + \cos\varphi \cos\delta \cos H
$$

</EqTip>

Every term is a sine or a cosine, which is exactly what the board's two edges read. Work it the
way the muvakkit did:

</div>

The $\varphi$ terms never change if you never move, which is why both labels say the boards were
made for the 41st parallel, "on which Istanbul also lies". Next to them is an 1848 study sheet, a
quadrant drawn in ink, on which someone wrote, roughly, "quadrant drawing, but faulty" (museum
label).

The quadrant gives a reading, not a running clock. Between two readings, something has to tick.

## Verge and foliot: a clock that ticks by itself

Mechanical clocks appear in Europe in the late 13th century. In 1271 Robertus Anglicus wrote that
clockmakers were trying to make one and had not yet succeeded
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verge_escapement)). The answer was the verge and
foliot: a crown-shaped wheel turned by a weight, and a bar on a vertical rod whose two flags let
the wheel go one tooth at a time.

<div class="fx-figure fx-wide">
  <VergePlate client:visible />
</div>

The first ones are thought to have drifted by one to two hours a day, the best by the mid-1600s
by about 15 minutes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verge_escapement)). In Istanbul,
Taqi al-Din wrote a book on mechanical clocks in 1559
([BEA, McGill](https://islamsci.mcgill.ca/RASI/BEA/Taqi_al-Din_BEA.htm)) and later used one to time
the stars at his observatory: "We divided each minute into five seconds"
([Muslim Heritage](https://muslimheritage.com/instruments-istanbul-observatory/)).

The trouble is in the plate's last step. A foliot has no rhythm of its own: it swings as fast as
it is pushed, so anything that changes the push changes the time.

## The pendulum: a clock with its own rhythm

A pendulum has its own rhythm. Galileo put it in writing in 1602: for small swings, the time of a
swing does not depend on how far it swings
([Museo Galileo](https://museogalileo.it/en/galileo/life.html)). Christiaan Huygens built the
first pendulum clock in 1656, and it erred by "less than 1 minute a day"
([NIST](https://www.nist.gov/node/446006)). The period depends on the length and on gravity, and
on almost nothing else:

<EqTip note="T is the time of one full swing, there and back. L is the length from the pivot to the bob's centre, g the acceleration of gravity, about 9.81 m/s². Four times the length doubles the period; the weight of the bob does not appear.">

$$
T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}
$$

</EqTip>

The escapement below is the deadbeat, first made by Thomas Tompion to a design by Richard
Towneley in 1675 and made popular by George Graham around 1715
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escapement)). Its pallets hold the wheel dead still
while the pendulum swings on, so the clock barely disturbs the thing that keeps its time.

<div class="fx-figure fx-wide">
  <PendulumPlate client:visible />
</div>

At 0.994 m the period is 2 seconds, one beat a second
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pendulum_clock)). Mustafa Şem'i's clock in the photo is
a weight-driven pendulum clock of this family. Its weakness is in that formula: the rod's length,
which heat changes, and $g$, which, it turns out, is not the same everywhere.

## The pendulum at sea, and Harrison's answer

At sea, time is longitude. The Earth "turns through one degree of longitude every four minutes"
([Royal Museums Greenwich](https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/time/what-longitude)), so a ship that
carries home-port time and compares it with local noon knows how far east or west it has come.
Britain's Longitude Act of 1714 offered £20,000 for longitude to within half a degree on a voyage
to the West Indies, about six weeks
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude_rewards)). Half a degree is two minutes of
time.

Newton told Parliament why no clock could do it: "the motion of a Ship, the variation of heat &
cold, wet and dry, and the difference of Gravity in different Latitudes"
([RMG](https://rmg.co.uk/stories/blog/newton-has-his-say)). Huygens had already learned at sea
that "the pendulum clock would never work accurately on a heaving ship's deck"
([Smithsonian](https://timeandnavigation.si.edu/navigating-at-sea/longitude-problem/solving-longitude-problem/early-sea-clocks)).
Here are all three of Newton's enemies, one at a time:

<div class="fx-figure fx-wide">
  <SeaClockPlate client:visible />
</div>

John Harrison's way out was to drop the pendulum. His first sea clock, H1, used two linked
balances swinging against each other so the ship's lurches cancelled
([RMG](https://www.rmg.co.uk/discover/explore/longitude-found-john-harrison)). His fourth, H4, was
a large watch whose balance "ticks five times a second" (same source). A balance wheel is pulled
back by a spring, not by gravity, so $g$ drops out of its period:

<EqTip note="I is the balance wheel's moment of inertia (its mass, and how far out that mass sits), κ the stiffness of the spiral spring. No g: heave and latitude do not change it. Heat still does, through both I and κ, which is why chronometers need temperature compensation.">

$$
T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{I}{\kappa}}
$$

</EqTip>

On the 1761 trial to Jamaica, after 81 days and 5 hours at sea and with its known rate allowed
for, H4 was 5 seconds slow: about one nautical mile of longitude
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Harrison)). After a second trial the Board of
Longitude agreed it had "kept time within the most stringent limits of the 1714 Act"
([RMG](https://www.rmg.co.uk/discover/explore/longitude-found-john-harrison)). I first ran into
this story at the Royal Observatory in Greenwich, which is why this section exists.

The limit now is the metal. A balance and a spring are hand-made, expensive, and still change
with heat and age.

## Quartz: a crystal that rings

The next oscillator is not a moving part you can see. In 1927 Warren Marrison and J. W. Horton at
Bell Labs built a clock around a vibrating quartz crystal
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warren_Marrison)). In 1969 Seiko put one on a wrist,
the Astron, good to about five seconds a month
([IEEE Milestone](https://ethw.org/Milestones:Electronic_Quartz_Wristwatch,_1969)).

<div class="fx-figure fx-wide">
  <QuartzPlate client:visible />
</div>

Watch crystals ring at 32,768 Hz because it is a power of two: fifteen halvings make one pulse a
second ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartz_clock)). They are cut to be truest near
25 °C and drift with temperature and age
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_oscillator)). Every crystal is a little
different, so no two quartz clocks agree for long.

## Atomic clocks: counting caesium

What quartz lacks is a frequency nature fixes for everyone. In 1955 Louis Essen and Jack Parry at
NPL built "the first successful atomic clock", good to a second in 300 years
([Science Museum Group](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co855/caesium-atomic-clock-1955)).

<div class="fx-figure fx-wide">
  <AtomicPlate client:visible />
</div>

Since 1967 the second has been defined as 9,192,631,770 periods of that caesium radiation
([BIPM](https://www.bipm.org/committees/cg/cgpm/13-1967/resolution-1)). JILA's strontium clock
"would neither gain nor lose one second in some 15 billion years"
([NIST](https://www.nist.gov/node/392596)). At that point the wall is not the clock: very little
needs time this good. One thing that does is in your pocket.

## GPS: time is distance

Your phone finds itself by timing radio signals from satellites about 20,200 km up. Light covers
about 30 cm in a nanosecond, so the distance to a satellite is a clock reading in disguise:

<EqTip note="d is the distance to the satellite, c the speed of light (299,792,458 m/s, exact by definition), Δt how long the signal took. An error of 3 ns in Δt is about 1 m.">

$$
d = c\,\Delta t
$$

</EqTip>

It is Harrison's problem again with the speeds changed: an error of 3 nanoseconds is about a metre
([Ashby](https://www.aapt.org/doorway/tgru/articles/ashbyarticle.pdf)).

<div class="fx-figure fx-wide">
  <GpsPlate client:visible />
</div>

Up there relativity gets a vote: "A GPS satellite clock will gain about 38 microseconds per day"
([GPS World](https://www.gpsworld.com/inside-the-box-gps-and-relativity/)), which left alone would
grow into range errors of up to about 11 km between daily corrections. So the satellite clocks are
set to tick at 10.22999999543 MHz instead of 10.23 MHz on the ground, and run right in orbit
(same source).

Every rung, best clock of its time against how far it drifts in a day:

Most of the climb happened in the last 400 years, and each step was one specific failure fixed:
no sun, an uneven drip, no running count, no rhythm, a moving floor, metal, a crystal that differs
from every other one.

measured on a rolling deck. That is the next post:
