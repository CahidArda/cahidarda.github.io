about an Ottoman engineer called Feyzi Efendi. In 1805 he wrote a treatise on measuring
altitudes with a sextant, and according to the panel he did it because of "his interest in a
box" he had found in the palace treasury.

A sextant measures one angle: how high the sun (or a star) stands above the horizon. With that
angle and a table, a sailor could say how far north of the equator he was to about a mile. This
post is the chain of instruments that led to the box, each one built because the last one hit a
wall.

## The problem: latitude is an angle

Cut the Earth through the poles and your position, and you get the drawing that sits beside
every plate below. Your latitude φ is the angle at the Earth's centre between the equator and
you. You can't measure it there, but you can measure its twin in the sky. The pole star stands
φ above your horizon; the noon sun stands 90° − φ above it, shifted by how far north or south of
the equator the sun is that day (its declination δ). One degree of error is 60 nautical miles
([NPS](https://home.nps.gov/fora/learn/education/navigation-and-related-instruments-in-16th-century-england.htm)),
so the whole story is about measuring one angle more finely, on ground that moves more.

## A stick and its shadow

Around 240 BC Eratosthenes, in Alexandria, heard that at noon on midsummer day the sun shone
straight down a well at Syene, far up the Nile. In Alexandria a bowl-shaped sundial still cast a
shadow, and Cleomedes says "the arc in the bowl is found to be one-fiftieth of its proper circle"
([Cleomedes, tr. Heath](https://roger-pearse.com/weblog/2017/04/15/cleomedes-how-big-is-the-earth)).
The plate does it with a plain stick, which is the same geometry.

<EqTip note="C is the circumference of the Earth, θ the shadow angle in degrees, and d the distance between the two cities. 360/θ says how many such slices fit in a full circle.">

$$
C = \frac{360^\circ}{\theta}\, d
$$

</EqTip>

With 5,000 stadia between the cities he got 250,000 stadia. Nobody knows his stadion, and "the
exact value of the stadion remains a subject of debate to this day"
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth%27s_circumference)), so the result is
anywhere from under 1% to 15% off. The angle held up better: Alexandria and Aswan differ by about
7.1°. The method's wall is in the last step: a stick needs level, still ground, and a ship has
neither.

## Polaris and the kamal

At sea you need an angle that needs no ground. The pole star gives one: it sits almost exactly
over the Earth's axis, so its height above the horizon is your latitude. Arab and Indian Ocean
navigators measured it with a kamal, a small board on a knotted cord (the Mariners' Museum dates
it 900 to 1900 CE). The cord is held "between his teeth" and the board at arm's length, lower edge
on the horizon, upper edge on the star
([Mariners' Museum](https://exploration.marinersmuseum.org/?p=409)). A knot for each home port
let a navigator sail north or south until the star sat right, then hold that latitude.

<div class="fx-figure fx-wide">
  <KamalProcedure client:visible />
</div>

Polaris is not quite at the pole: 0.66° away in 2018, creeping closer until just after 2100
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polaris)). Forget the circle and you are off by up to
40 miles. The worse wall is in the plate's last step: the further north you sail, the shorter the
cord and the more crowded the knots, and south of the equator Polaris is gone.

## The cross-staff: any angle, staring at the sun

The sun is up everywhere, so the next instrument measured the noon sun with a ruled scale. The
cross-staff, described by Levi ben Gerson in 1342 ([Museo Galileo](https://redi.imss.fi.it/inventions/index.php/Cross-Staff)),
is a staff about a metre long with sliding crosspieces. The Portuguese were using it at sea by
around 1515 ([Mariners' Museum](https://exploration.marinersmuseum.org/?p=205)). The kamal's
board became a transom, and the cord a graduated staff.

<div class="fx-figure fx-wide">
  <CrossStaffProcedure client:visible />
</div>

The wall is your eye. You look straight at the noon sun ("a piece of smoked glass was frequently
used", [NPS](https://home.nps.gov/fora/learn/education/navigation-and-related-instruments-in-16th-century-england.htm)),
and at the horizon 37° below it, at the same moment. Navigators "could lose their eyesight from
staring at the sun" ([Mariners' Museum](https://exploration.marinersmuseum.org/?p=205)).

## The backstaff: turn your back on the sun

Sailors started using the cross-staff backwards, reading its shadow instead, and John Davis
turned that into the backstaff around 1594, while looking for the Northwest Passage
([Mariners' Museum](https://exploration.marinersmuseum.org/object/back-staff/)). You face away
from the sun, and a shadow vane throws a line onto a slit you look through at the horizon. One
look, no glare, and a big 30° arc for fine readings.

<div class="fx-figure fx-wide">
  <BackstaffProcedure client:visible />
</div>

It "can really only take measurements using the sun"
([Mariners' Museum](https://exploration.marinersmuseum.org/object/back-staff/)): no shadow at night,
none in haze. And like every staff, it depends on your hand holding it to the horizon while the
deck moves.

## The mariner's astrolabe: let gravity hold it

The Portuguese tried the opposite idea from about 1470: forget the horizon and let gravity give
the vertical. The mariner's astrolabe is a heavy brass ring hung from a shackle, "heavier than the
land version so it would stay steady", with "parts of the disc cut away to reduce wind resistance"
([Royal Museums Greenwich](https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/space-astronomy/what-mariners-astrolabe)).
You never look at the sun: you turn the alidade until sunlight through one pinhole falls on the
other. It is the planispheric astrolabe stripped down to its rim and its sighting bar; the full
instrument, which is a whole sky in brass, has [its own post](/articles/instruments/astrolabe).

<div class="fx-figure fx-wide">
  <MarinerAstrolabeProcedure client:visible />
</div>

Peter de Hilster tested replicas and concluded that "an accuracy of 10 arc minutes is feasible"
for a good observer, with novices at sea nearer 30′
([de Hilster 2014](https://www.dehilster.info/docs/MarinersMirror_Hilster_Mariners_Astrolabe%2C2014.pdf)).
Ten miles is the good case. The wall: a hanging ring swings with the ship, and gravity is no
steadier than the deck.

## The sextant: two mirrors

On 13 May 1731 John Hadley's "new instrument for taking angles" was read to the Royal Society
([Royal Society](https://makingscience.royalsociety.org/items/rbo_17_72/description-of-a-new-instrument-for-taking-angles-by-john-hadley));
Thomas Godfrey, a Philadelphia glazier, built the same thing at about the same time
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octant_%28instrument%29)). Hadley's octant had a 45°
arc; in 1757 John Bird made the first sextant, with 60°
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflecting_instrument)). It fixes every earlier wall
at once. You look at the horizon through the clear half of a fixed mirror and see the sun, through
shades, in the silvered half. One look, no glare, any star, and no gravity to swing.

<div class="fx-figure fx-wide">
  <SextantModel n={6} client:visible />
</div>

Why does the deck not matter? Both mirrors move together, and two reflections turn the light by
twice the angle between the mirrors, whatever angle it came in at. That is also why a 60° arc is
numbered to 120.

<EqTip note="θ is the angle between the two mirrors (how far the index arm has turned from zero). D is how much the light leaving the second mirror is bent away from the light arriving at the first. It does not depend on the angle the light came in at.">

$$
D = 2\theta
$$

</EqTip>

<div class="fx-figure">
  <DoubleReflection client:visible />
</div>

In the eyepiece "the relative position of the two images will remain steady"
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sextant)), and a good sextant reads to a tenth of an
arcminute. Feyzi Efendi's 1805 treatise explains this instrument "with the aid of logarithm"
(exhibition panel); Feza Günergun's chapter on it calls the sextant an *alla franca*, European,
instrument ([Günergun 2019](https://avesis.istanbul.edu.tr/yayin/84263c24-57f0-45b0-bf7e-486d3895ff5c/scientific-instruments-between-east-and-west)).

## The noon sight, step by step

Here is the whole procedure with a sextant, from waiting for noon to the latitude, with the small
corrections a real navigator makes: the horizon sits a little low because your eye is above the
sea, you measured the sun's edge and not its centre, and the air bends its light.

<EqTip note="φ is your latitude, h the sun's corrected height above the horizon at noon, and δ the sun's declination for the date (positive when the sun is north of the equator). This form is for an observer north of the sun, who sees it due south at noon.">

$$
\varphi = 90^\circ - h + \delta
$$

</EqTip>

## Why the sun's height changes

Step 5 of that plate was "look up δ". Why does it change at all? The Earth's axis is tilted, so the
sun's yearly path (the ecliptic) is tilted about 23.44° to the celestial equator
([USNO](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/faq/sun_approx)). The sun spends half the year north of the
equator and half south, which is why the noon sun is high in June and low in December, and why
every plate above needs a table for the date.

## Latitude is solved. Longitude needs a clock

With a sextant and an almanac, latitude was a daily routine. Longitude was not. The Earth turns
15° an hour, so longitude is a difference in time: four minutes wrong is a whole degree wrong. The
sextant could measure the Moon against the stars and use it as a clock
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_distance_%28navigation%29)), but the real answer
was a clock that kept time at sea, which is the story in
[the clocks post](/articles/instruments/time).

## No horizon: aircraft, spacecraft, and the stars overhead

Aircraft have no sea horizon, so bubble sextants used a spirit level, and got worse: about 2′ on
land in skilled hands, 5 to 10 miles in flight
([Bowditch, ch. 14](https://thenauticalalmanac.com/2019_Bowditch-_American_Practical_Navigator/Volume-_1/05-%20Part%203-%20Celestial%20Navigation/Chapter%2014-%20Instruments%20For%20Celestial%20Navigation.pdf)).
The bubble feels the plane's acceleration, so "many readings be taken and then averaged"
([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bubble_octant)). Spacecraft dropped the horizon too:
a star tracker matches the star pattern it sees against a catalogue, and a CubeSat-sized one
points to about 30 arcseconds ([AAC Clyde ST200](https://www.aac-clyde.space/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/ST200.pdf)).

On the ground the same idea turned into the most precise latitude instrument there is. A digital
zenith camera is levelled with tilt meters, photographs the stars straight overhead and matches
them against a catalogue; the declination of the point overhead is your latitude. Its job today is
the gap between that latitude and the one GPS gives, "better than 0.1 arc sec"
([Hirt et al. 2010](https://forschungsportal.hcu-hamburg.de/en/publications/modern-determination-of-vertical-deflections-using-digital-zenith-4/)).
Istanbul Technical University runs one, at about 0.3″
([Albayrak et al. 2019](https://research.itu.edu.tr/en/publications/the-use-of-the-automated-digital-zenith-camera-system-in-istanbul/)).

The catalogues behind all this got better too. Hipparcos (1989 to 1993) measured 118,218 stars at
the milliarcsecond level ([ESA](https://cosmos.esa.int/web/hipparcos/the-mission)), and Gaia went
"down to microarcsecond precision" ([Sky & Telescope](https://skyandtelescope.org/astronomy-news/galaxy-mapping-satellite-ends-science-operations/)).
Twenty microarcseconds is a 4 cm coin on the Moon. Eratosthenes' bowl was good to perhaps a tenth
of a degree: a factor of about twenty million, all in the same quantity.
