I built a small app called [Dikilitaş](https://obelisk-390.vercel.app/), after the Turkish name of the Obelisk of
Theodosius in Istanbul. You turn a 3D model of the obelisk, tap a line of
hieroglyphs, and read what it says. The pedestal is there too, with its reliefs
and its Latin and Greek inscriptions.

## Where this came from

I was on a tour of Sultanahmet with a guide. At the
[German Fountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Fountain), the octagonal
fountain Wilhelm II gifted the city after his 1898 visit, our guide stopped and
read the Ottoman inscriptions to us. The dome is covered in gold mosaic, with the tughra
of Abdülhamid II and the monogram of Wilhelm II set in medallions, and a band of
calligraphy runs along the arches.

its marble pedestal has a Latin inscription on one side and a Greek one on the
other. Our guide told us its history, but the texts themselves went unread.

Still, I was standing in front of a
[3,400-year-old text](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obelisk_of_Theodosius),
carved for the temple of Karnak under Thutmose III and moved here by Theodosius I
in 390, and I could not read a word of it. Wouldn't it be nice if there was an app for that?

If you read my post on [Bosphore 1819](/articles/bosphore-1819), you know where
this is going. That one started with an old map on a café wall and ended with
an app that reads every label on it. Same itch, different artifact.

## Prompt one: four photos

I took a photo of each face of the obelisk and handed them to Claude with a
short description: a minimal app, a 3D model you can turn, translations when
you hover over the text, and it should work on my phone.

## Prompt two: make it feel ancient

The second prompt took the app from 25 percent done to 90 percent done, in my
eyes. I wanted the page to feel ancient, like something the architect of the
obelisk could have unrolled in front of the pharaoh.

So I went looking for a feeling, not a spec. I searched for photos of
hieroglyphs and picked one I liked. Then I added a photo I had taken at the
[Rahmi M. Koç Museum](https://www.rmk-museum.org.tr/) in Istanbul: a wall of
framed prints of steam traction engines, each one drawn flat, from the side, in
clean colour with thin ink lines, a title on top and a small table of
specifications underneath. I love that museum, and these prints are easy to walk
past next to the ships, trains and planes, but they are one of the main reasons I
keep going back. I asked Claude to combine the two.

The prompt itself was short. Roughly: if the model is not extremely realistic, it
looks like a game artifact; look at these prints, they are flat but they read as
real; can we have that aesthetic and still turn it? And one more idea: a zoomed
out view that keeps the print look, and a zoomed in view where the text reads
like a page you can scroll, which also suits a phone.

This time it nailed it.

The look comes from very little. The obelisk is a real 3D model in
[three.js](https://threejs.org/), seen through an
[orthographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthographic_projection) camera so nothing
shrinks with distance, the way an engineering drawing has no perspective. There
is no lighting model at all. Every face gets one flat colour, computed from a
single fixed light, and an ink outline drawn along its edges. That is the whole
trick behind the "print" feeling.

<div class="fx-figure">
  <FlatShade client:visible />
</div>

Around it sits a cream page with a title block and a small table of
specifications, borrowed straight from the engine prints: height, material,
where it was carved, where it was raised. The hieroglyphs on the model are real
Unicode characters from the
[Egyptian Hieroglyphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_Hieroglyphs_%28Unicode_block%29)
block, set in Google's Noto Sans Egyptian Hieroglyphs and painted onto the faces.
The reading page uses the same characters, so the column you read on the right
is the column you see on the stone.

## Then, one thing at a time

From there it was a long list of small asks. Centre the glyphs in their column.
Make the highlight follow the line I am reading. Keep the model still when the
card on mobile changes height. Stop the camera from going under the ground. Turn
the obelisk slowly when the page opens, and stop as soon as I touch it. Four
languages. Smoother zoom. None of these were hard. They were just mine to notice.

The pedestal took the most rounds. The first drawings were grey pill shapes
standing in rows. What worked was measuring: each relief was flattened from a
photograph and every figure placed from it, so no two spectators are the same,
just like on the stone.

## The stack

Very light. One HTML file, three.js from a CDN, and fonts from Google Fonts: EB
Garamond for the text, Noto Serif JP for Japanese, Noto Sans Egyptian Hieroglyphs
for the glyphs. The pedestal drawings are several hundred lines of canvas code.
There is no framework, no npm install and no build step. You can serve the folder
with anything.

It was built and previewed in an [Upstash Box](https://upstash.com/docs/box)
from one Claude Code session, the same setup as the Bosphore app: the agent
edited the files in the box, took screenshots with a headless browser to check
its own work, and pushed to GitHub from there.

## In the end

This one will probably get less attention than the Bosphore map. With the map
you can look up the street you live on. With this one you can read an obelisk,
which is a smaller crowd, unless there are ancient Egyptians among my followers.

I am happy with it anyway. Next time I walk past the Hippodrome I can read what
Thutmose III had carved, and what Theodosius had written underneath about
getting it to stand up again.
