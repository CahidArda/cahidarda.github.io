Frontier models have started to specialize. GPT is strong at math and planning, Opus at software
engineering and security, Gemini at science and recall — and no single one wins everywhere. [Sakana
AI's Fugu](https://sakana.ai/fugu) asks the obvious next question: what if a model's job was not to
answer your query, but to decide _which other models_ should, and how they should work together?

> "Sakana Fugu is a family of language models trained to adaptively and dynamically orchestrate a team
> of more powerful frontier agent workers... the user interacts with Fugu as if calling a single model,
> while internally the system can route, delegate, and coordinate across multiple specialized agents."
> — [Sakana Fugu Technical Report](https://sakana.ai/fugu)

Fugu treats orchestration as "a new complementary scaling axis beyond ever larger and expensive
language models." This post is a tour of how that works, built around the two ICLR 2026 papers the
system is grounded in: [**TRINITY**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.04695), an _evolved_ coordinator, and
the [**Conductor**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.04388), an _RL-trained_ one. The diagrams below are
interactive — play them.

## One model, many minds

There are two variants. **Fugu** picks a single best worker per query, so its latency is "comparable to
a direct call to a frontier model." **Fugu-Ultra** composes a whole team. Toggle between them:

The trick that makes this more than a router is that Fugu is itself a _trained_ model. It is not a
hand-written `if coding: use_opus` rule; it learns, from data, who is good at what.

## A pool of specialists

Why bother? Because the workers genuinely differ, and the differences are fine-grained:

> "At the domain level, past GPT-series models often displayed state-of-the-art performance on
> mathematical reasoning tasks, while Opus-series models have specialized in software engineering and
> cybersecurity... in competitive coding, Gemini-3.1-Pro can be particularly effective at directly
> implementing known algorithms, while GPT-series models can often excel at planning and combining
> multiple algorithmic ideas."

A good orchestrator should _track_ these specializations and deploy each model where it shines. Fugu
does: its routing distribution shifts by domain, peaking on whichever worker is SOTA there. Pick a
domain and watch the mix move (the percentages are illustrative of the routing trends the

## Fugu: routing in a single forward pass

Fugu builds on **TRINITY**. The orchestrator is a small (~0.6B) language model, but it never writes the
answer. A tiny head — about **ten thousand parameters** — reads one hidden state and emits a logit per
worker. The policy is just a softmax over those logits:

$$
\pi_\theta(a \mid s) \;\propto\; \exp\!\big(f_\theta(h(s))_a\big)
$$

> "Our method only relies on the head's logit outputs, and the coordinator's generated text is
> discarded because the job of prompting is delegated to the LLMs in the pool... this allows the
> coordinator to take hidden states corresponding to an earlier token instead of the penultimate to
> make a quick decision." — [TRINITY](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.04695)

This _decision-only_ design is the whole reason Fugu is fast. TRINITY also assigns each chosen worker
one of three roles — **Thinker**, **Worker**, **Verifier** — and loops over several turns; production
Fugu drops the roles to shrink the decision space further. Toggle between the two below:

## Training without gradients

How do you train a head whose decision only pays off after several expensive LLM calls? In two stages.
First, supervised fine-tuning on single-step tasks, where every worker is scored and the targets are a
_soft_ distribution over who did best — not a single hard label:

$$
p_i(j) = \frac{\exp(\bar r_{i,j}/\tau)}{\sum_{j'} \exp(\bar r_{i,j'}/\tau)}, \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_i \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(p_i(\cdot)\,\|\,\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid q_i)\big)
$$

Then, the orchestrator is refined on full end-to-end tasks with a derivative-free evolution strategy,
directly maximizing the terminal reward $J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}[R(\tau)]$ by
sampling perturbed parameter vectors $\theta^{(k)} = \theta_t + \sigma_t D_t z^{(k)}$ and recombining
the fittest. TRINITY found this beats reinforcement learning in its regime:

> "We observe weak coupling among parameters — each has only a tiny influence on the scalar reward,
> making traditional methods like REINFORCE's per-parameter gradients low-SNR and therefore
> ineffective... a derivative-free Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy is effective in this
> particular regime: high dimensionality, weak parameter correlations, and high per-step cost."

## Fugu-Ultra: conducting an orchestra

For the hardest problems, a single pick is not enough. **Fugu-Ultra** builds on the **Conductor**, which
emits an _entire agentic workflow as natural language_ — and is trained end-to-end with reinforcement
learning (GRPO):

> "The Conductor outputs full agentic workflows as natural language that divide an input task, allocate
> arbitrary subtasks, and define targeted communication strategies to best make use of the agents'
> complementary capabilities." — [the Conductor](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.04388)

Concretely, the Conductor produces three aligned lists: the **subtasks**, the **worker** for each, and an
**access list** stating which earlier outputs each worker is allowed to see. Those access lists _are_ the
communication topology — letting the same model emit a chain, a best-of-N vote, a debate tree, or even
a recursive call to itself. The reward is brutally simple: $0$ if the lists don't parse, $0.5$ for a
well-formed-but-wrong workflow, $1$ for a correct one, with the GRPO advantage normalized within each
group, $A_i = \big(r_i - \mathrm{mean}(r)\big)/\mathrm{std}(r)$. Switch topologies and step through one:

## The strategies that emerge

Nothing above hand-codes "use a debate tree for trivia." These patterns _emerge_ from reward
maximization, and the [technical report's](https://sakana.ai/fugu) qualitative analysis reads like a set
of habits a good engineering manager would develop. **Adaptive aggregation:** on a niche trivia question Fugu-Ultra built a tree with
Gemini synthesizing two attempts, but on a math question it swapped GPT into the same root role —

> "dynamic adaptation of an aggregator role is precisely the kind of adaptation unavailable to existing
> multi-agent systems, which necessitate a fixed model to always act as a final synthesizer."

**Build-and-debug:** GPT builds, Opus reviews. On one Terminal Bench task, after GPT built a PyPI
server, "Opus was then deployed to enumerate all concrete risks in GPT's implementation," caught three
real bugs and a bogus reachability check, and "relaying these findings back to GPT enabled GPT to
complete the build successfully." **Bring in a specialist:** for a cryptanalysis problem Fugu-Ultra had
Opus write the attack, then tasked GPT "to act specifically as a math specialist to re-derive the entire
attack from first principles." Different jobs, different experts, decided per query.

## Does it work?

Orchestration alone is enough to match or beat every individual worker in the pool, and to trade blows
with frontier models that aren't even _in_ the pool. Browse the benchmarks:

<div class="fx-figure">
  <BenchmarkChart client:visible />
</div>

It generalizes past static benchmarks too. On Karpathy's AutoResearch — an agent autonomously tuning a
GPT training recipe over 123 experiments — "Fugu-Ultra achieves the lowest mean validation BPB
(0.9774 ± 0.0019)," ahead of all three frontier baselines, "an initial demonstration that multi-model
orchestration can outperform any individual frontier agent on an agentic training-optimization
benchmark."

The bigger bet is architectural: because Fugu "composes models at the behavioral level rather than the
parameter level, it can incorporate new worker models as they become available" — no retraining, no
weight access, just a new name in the pool. If it holds, frontier capability stops being something only
the largest training runs can buy.
