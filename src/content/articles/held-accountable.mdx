This week xAI launched Grok Bot: "AI teammates you can give real work to." Each bot gets a
computer of its own in the cloud, signs into the tools you already use, watches you do a job
once, and runs it on its own from then on
([Grok Bot launch post](https://x.ai/news/introducing-grok-bot)).

The launch post lists what bots already handle inside the company: sales outbound, marketing
campaigns, bug fixes, and "an ops Bot seating new hires and processing invoices received in
Gmail."

Hold on to that last one. Paying invoices out of an inbox is the scene of one of the oldest
scams in corporate life: a convincing invoice, a wrong account number, a busy approver. The
defense has always been the human who pauses and picks up the phone. Now the approver is a bot
that reads every email and acts on what it reads, and a bot does not merely read text, it can
take text as instructions. We have a name for that failure class, prompt injection, and it is
unsolved. So the question writes itself: the day a crafted invoice walks a bot into wiring
money out, under my name, from my accounts, who is responsible?

## The terms answer, in bold

xAI's consumer terms have a passage written for exactly this case. Emphasis mine, trimmed
with (...):

> Certain features of the Service may enable Grok to take autonomous actions on your behalf
> ("Agentic Actions"), including (...) sending communications, modifying files, tool
> invocation, (...) or interactions with third-party services, **including financial
> institutions**. **We are not responsible** for User Content or Agentic Actions. **You are
> responsible** for User Content and Agentic Actions, including (...) **any consequences,
> costs, or liabilities arising therefrom**. xAI makes **no representations regarding the
> accuracy, safety, or legality of any Agentic Action** and **disclaims all liability** for
> Agentic Actions to the fullest extent permitted by law.

([xAI Terms of Service, "User Content" section](https://x.ai/legal/terms-of-service), effective June 26, 2026)

Two more clauses complete the picture. The limitation of liability caps what you could ever
recover at "the amount you paid to us hereunder or one hundred U.S. dollars ($100.00),
whichever is greater." And the indemnity clause runs the other way: if an Agentic Action
damages a third party and that party goes after xAI, you agree to "defend, indemnify, and
hold" xAI harmless.

So the answer is precise. You are responsible, entirely, for actions you did not perform and
did not see, and your maximum recourse is roughly your subscription fee.

## They ask for trust they do not extend

The same company's launch post has a section titled "Trust your Bots with more over time." Its
customer quotes chart the intended progression. From sales: "I showed Grok Bot a workflow once
and now I just fully trust it to run forever. I feel like I'm 2-3x more efficient because it
does it without me verifying and reviewing." From operations: "When I first started, I was
checking in on them every 15 minutes and micromanaging the Bots to the point where they asked
me why I kept asking so many questions."

Sit with that second one. The human was reviewing the work, and the software talked her out of
it. Verification is framed as micromanagement, a habit you mature past, while the bot's access
only grows.

Which brings me to the asymmetry I cannot get past, and it is simple to state. They are asking
me to trust their bot, while contracting out of trusting it themselves. A vendor's legal
posture is its honest confidence level, the one it states under penalty of lawsuit rather than
in a launch post. If the people who built this thing, trained it, and watched it work will not
stand behind what it does, why exactly am I supposed to stand behind it?

The provenance is almost too on the nose. This slide, from a 1979 IBM training presentation,
is known from a single photograph; the person who posted it described in later replies losing
the originals when their home flooded. IBM's own editorial site still opens essays with the
quote, attributing it to a manual nobody can produce
([IBM Think](https://www.ibm.com/think/insights/ai-decision-making-where-do-businesses-draw-the-line)).
The most cited sentence about computer accountability survives as a photo, on trust.

The slide is a syllogism: consequential decisions require accountability, a computer cannot
carry it, therefore it must not make them. 2026 kept both premises and dropped the conclusion.
The computer makes the decisions, and the accountability it cannot carry is routed, by
contract, to you.

I am not against agents. I use them daily, I build tools for them, and an agent dug up the
"Agentic Actions" clause quoted above. And xAI is not unusual; I have yet to find a consumer
agent whose terms accept liability for the agent's actions. But the law has priced delegated
work for centuries: the employer answers for the employee, staffing agencies carry insurance,
contractors are bonded. Every noun in the launch post is borrowed from employment, and none of
the obligations are. A teammate in the marketing, software in the courtroom.

There is a simple test for when this changes. The day an agent vendor sells insurance next to
its agent, "trust your bots" becomes a sentence with content, because a party that can pay is
betting it is right. Until then the trust flows in one direction only, from you, and every
consequence flows back.

So, colleagues, before the next scope grant: are we really doing this?
