This week xAI launched Grok Bot: "AI teammates you can give real work to." Each bot gets a
computer of its own in the cloud. It signs into the tools you already use. To teach it a
workflow, you let it watch you do the job once: "It watches the steps and remembers how you
like the work done." ([Grok Bot launch post](https://x.ai/news/introducing-grok-bot))

The engineering is impressive, and my feeds were full of people saying so, me included. But I
kept getting stuck on one boring question. If I hand a bot my Stripe and one of its runs breaks
something, real money, gone: who is responsible?

## The terms already answer this

xAI thought about my question before I asked it. Their consumer terms have a passage written
for exactly this case:

> Certain features of the Service may enable Grok to take autonomous actions on your behalf
> ("Agentic Actions"), including but not limited to web browsing, code execution, sending
> communications, modifying files, tool invocation, data processing, or interactions with
> third-party services, including financial institutions. We are not responsible for User
> Content or Agentic Actions. You are responsible for User Content and Agentic Actions,
> including ensuring that it does not violate any applicable law or these Terms and any
> consequences, costs, or liabilities arising therefrom. xAI makes no representations
> regarding the accuracy, safety, or legality of any Agentic Action and disclaims all
> liability for Agentic Actions to the fullest extent permitted by law.

([xAI Terms of Service, "User Content" section](https://x.ai/legal/terms-of-service), effective June 26, 2026)

Two more clauses complete the picture. The limitation of liability caps what you could ever
recover at "the amount you paid to us hereunder or one hundred U.S. dollars ($100.00),
whichever is greater." And the indemnity clause runs the other way: if an Agentic Action
damages a third party and that party goes after xAI, you agree to "defend, indemnify, and
hold" xAI harmless.

So the answer to the Stripe question is precise. You are responsible, entirely, for actions
you did not perform and did not see. Your maximum recourse is roughly your subscription fee.

Notice the mechanism that makes this airtight: the bot has no identity of its own inside your
systems. It signs in as you, on purpose; the pitch is that bots "can sign in and work across
apps, tools, and websites, including platforms with no clean API or MCP." Every audit log,
every chargeback dispute, every access review will show one actor, and it is you. An IBM essay
on AI decision-making quotes the consultant Guy Pearce: "shared accountability often leads to
no accountability" ([IBM Think](https://www.ibm.com/think/insights/ai-decision-making-where-do-businesses-draw-the-line)).
What we have here is tidier than that. Nothing gets diluted. The accountability arrives fully
formed, routed around the party that built the actor and the actor itself, delivered to the
one party who did not perform the action.

## Trust as a growth curve

The launch post has a section titled "Trust your Bots with more over time." Its customer
quotes chart the intended progression. From sales: "I showed Grok Bot a workflow once and now
I just fully trust it to run forever. I feel like I'm 2-3x more efficient because it does it
without me verifying and reviewing." From operations: "When I first started, I was checking in
on them every 15 minutes and micromanaging the Bots to the point where they asked me why I
kept asking so many questions."

Sit with that second one. The human was reviewing the work, and the software talked her out of
it. Verification is framed as micromanagement, a habit you mature past. The product's success
curve is your review rate going to zero while its access grows.

Both curves are the advertised behavior, straight from the launch post. What no one advertises
is the widening area between them. The terms quoted above are the only document that names its
owner, and the owner is you.

## A slide from 1979

Engineers noticed this problem before agents existed. Before most of us existed. A 1979 IBM
training presentation contained a slide that has been circulating ever since:

> A COMPUTER CAN NEVER BE HELD ACCOUNTABLE
>
> THEREFORE A COMPUTER MUST NEVER MAKE A MANAGEMENT DECISION

The provenance is almost too on the nose. We know the slide from a single photograph, posted
in 2017 by the account above, who in later replies described losing the original materials
when their home flooded. IBM's own editorial site opens essays with the quote, attributing it
to a training manual nobody can produce. The most cited sentence about computer accountability
survives as a photo of a slide, on trust.

The slide is a syllogism. Consequential decisions require accountability; a computer cannot
carry it; therefore it must not make them. 2026 found a fourth path the authors did not list:
let the computer make the decisions anyway, and route the accountability it cannot carry to
whoever clicked "create your first teammate."

## The teammate test

Every noun in the launch post is borrowed from employment: teammate, colleague, coworker, a
"chief of staff" bot managing specialists, an ops bot "seating new hires." The law has handled
employees who break things for centuries. Respondeat superior: the employer answers for damage
an employee causes in the course of their work. That rule is why staffing agencies carry
insurance and contractors are bonded. Accountability for delegated work is a solved, priced
problem, everywhere except here.

To be fair, xAI is not unusual. I have yet to find a consumer agent whose terms accept
liability for the agent's actions; this is the industry's standard posture, inherited from an
era when software did exactly what you clicked and nothing else. The boilerplate did not
change. The claim did. The same kind of paragraph that once disclaimed responsibility for a
crashing spreadsheet now disclaims responsibility for an autonomous actor holding your logins,
your inbox, and your payment rails. A teammate in the marketing, software in the courtroom.

## Are we really doing this?

I am not against agents. I use them daily, I build tools for them, and an agent dug up the
"Agentic Actions" clause quoted above. I want this technology to exist.

But in every other corner of engineering, trust is a claim someone stands behind. Here is the
test I keep returning to: the day an agent vendor sells insurance alongside its agent, "trust
your bots" becomes a sentence with content, because a party that can pay is betting it is
right. Until then the trust flows in one direction only. You extend it. Nobody returns it.

We are the profession wiring these things in. We grant the OAuth scopes, we record the
workflows, we decide what the bot can touch. The people who made that slide in 1979 were not
afraid of computers; they were selling them. Read it again and it is less a limit on what
computers could do than a promise about what their makers would refuse to ask of them.

I would like our generation of builders to be able to make a promise like that. So, before the
next scope grant: are we really doing this?
