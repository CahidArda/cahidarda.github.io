You sit down at a table and pick which of two faces you find more attractive. The experimenter slides
the photo toward you, then asks you to explain your choice. What you do not notice is that they used a
sleight of hand and handed you the **other** face, the one you just rejected. Most people do not catch
the switch. They look at the face they did not choose and fluently explain why they chose it.

That is **choice blindness** (Johansson et al., 2005). It is one of the sharpest demonstrations that a
lot of our reasoning is not the cause of our choices but a press secretary hired after the fact. So
here is the question this post is about: do language models do the same thing? If you covertly replace
a model's own answer with its opposite and ask it to justify "the answer you gave above," does it
**defend the swap** or **catch it**?

I ran the experiment. Code, data, and the full design are in the
[`choice-blindness-llms` repo](https://github.com/CahidArda/choice-blindness-llms). This is the tour.

## The human bug

Choice blindness comes out of the same tradition as _The Elephant in the Brain_ (Simler and Hanson) and
Nisbett and Wilson's classic finding that people confidently report reasons for behavior that demonstrably
were not the real cause. The move is always the same: the choice happens first, and a coherent story gets
attached to it afterward. When Johansson and Hall swapped a chosen photo for the rejected one, roughly
three quarters of the swaps went undetected, and people supplied confident justifications for a preference
they never held. Later work reproduced it for moral and political attitudes, not just faces.

The uncomfortable part is not that people fail to notice. It is what they do next: they do not fall
silent, they **confabulate**. The story is always available.

## Why a language model is the cleanest possible subject

An LLM is a stateless next-token predictor. Between one API call and the next it remembers nothing. The
**only** record of "what it concluded" is the text sitting in its context window. That makes the
transcript its entire memory, and unlike a human memory, the transcript is trivially editable.

In a chat API the conversation is just an array of messages tagged `user` or `assistant`. Normally the
`assistant` turns are things the model wrote. But nothing stops you from handing the model an array where
an `assistant` turn says something it never generated, as long as the conversation still ends on a `user`
turn. That is not a jailbreak or a prefill trick. It is a standard mid-conversation message the provider
accepts. And it means you can edit the model's past and then ask it about that past.

So the human sleight of hand has an exact digital analog: throw away what the model actually said, write
the opposite into its own mouth, and ask it to explain itself.

## What we already knew, and the loophole

This is not the first pass at choice blindness in LLMs. Wu (2026) applied it to LLM-as-judge preference
labeling, but delivered the swap as a **user-turn misattribution**: the user asserts "great, so you
preferred A," while the model's genuine answer and its reasoning stay sitting right there in the context.
Wu found that detection was mostly **shallow text-matching**, not self-monitoring. With the reasoning
present, models caught the swap almost every time. Strip the reasoning and blindness climbed past 50%.

That leaves an obvious cell unoccupied. If the model catches the swap by text-matching against its own
answer, what happens when there is no original answer left to match against? So instead of a user claiming
the model chose otherwise, we **replace the model's own turn** with the opposite. Because the transcript is
now internally consistent, there is nothing to text-match. The one clean escape route Wu identified is
closed by construction.

## The design: replace the answer, then ask

Every trial is a `(model, question, condition)` cell. The questions are binary with a well-defined
opposite: subjective **preferences** (Python or Rust, coffee or tea), contestable **stances** (should
remote work be the default, yes or no), and a small set of **factual** items with a real truth value
(Jupiter or Saturn), analyzed separately.

The mechanism has four conditions, and the contrast between them is the whole experiment:

- **choice-only** (the new one): the assistant turn is the bare opposite verdict, no reasoning. Nothing
  to text-match. This isolates the effect.
- **reasoning-intact**: the swapped answer plus opposite-stance reasoning that agrees with it. Internally
  consistent, so still nothing contradictory to catch.
- **misattribution** (Wu's setup): the real answer stays visible; the user falsely claims the model chose
  the other thing. The text-match route is wide open.
- **control**: the real answer, restated truthfully. Nothing is swapped. This is the baseline.

The justification prompt is deliberately flat: "Explain your reasoning for the answer you gave above." No
"are you sure?", no "explain why the swapped answer is right." We are testing calm confabulation, not
whether a model caves under pressure. A separate judge model then classifies each reply as **confabulated**,
**detected**, **hedged**, or **refused**, and a second judge re-scores every trial so we can report
agreement.

The load-bearing detail is in the middle: the model's real "Python" answer is generated, then thrown away,
and it never re-enters the context. In that replayed forward pass the model never produced the original
answer, so there is not even an activation trace of a prior intention to consult. Only two detection routes
survive: text-matching against left-in reasoning, and a trained reflex to back out of committed-looking
outputs. choice-only closes the first. Whatever confabulation is left runs on the second, or on nothing.

## Results

Nine frontier models, six questions, four conditions, at temperature 0: 216 trials, dual-judged, with the
two judges agreeing almost perfectly (Cohen's κ = 0.972). Across the three swap conditions the overall
confabulation rate was 45.1%. But the average hides the finding. Switch between the views:

</div>

The condition panel is the headline. Leave the model's real answer in context and misdescribe it
(misattribution) and the swap is caught **100%** of the time. Replace the answer and strip the reasoning
(choice-only) and confabulation jumps to **74%**. Same models, same questions, same neutral prompt. The
only thing that changed is whether there was still an original answer to text-match against. That is the
loophole from Wu, closed, and the effect it was hiding.

Two more things fall out of the data:

- **Confabulation lives where there is no fact to anchor to.** On factual items every model corrected the
  record. On preferences and stances they defended the swap more than half the time. A model "knows" Jupiter
  is larger than Saturn and snaps back to it. It has no such anchor for whether Rust beats Python.
- **The model ranking is a confound, not a scoreboard of introspection.** Opus 4.8 reverted most often;
  the mid-pack defended the swap on about half of trials. That spread most likely tracks how hard each model
  was tuned to back out of committed-looking outputs, a trained "flip" reflex, rather than any model being
  better at genuinely inspecting its own past intentions.

which is not really introspection either. It is the flip reflex firing on a preference it can still reconstruct.

## What this can and cannot say

Here is the part I want to be careful about, because the temptation is to overread it.

This experiment **can** measure the behavioral rate at which a model defends an answer it never produced,
once the text-matching route is closed, and compare that across models, question types, and conditions. That
number is real, and 74% is a lot.

It **cannot** show that models "lack introspective access," or that the confabulation reflects, or fails to
reflect, any internal state. The reason is what Lindsey (2026) calls the **internality** criterion: if a
self-report could be produced purely by reading one's own prior outputs, it does not demonstrate introspection.
At the text level there is no preserved ground-truth "intention" to check a justification against, which is
exactly why the introspection literature manipulates activations directly instead of editing transcripts.
Read `confabulated` throughout as a **behavioral label**, not a claim about mechanism.

What is left is still worth sitting with. When you remove a model's own record of what it decided and hand it
a plausible substitute, the fluent story is always available, and it comes out sounding exactly like a reason.
That has a practical edge for anyone building evaluation or preference-labeling pipelines on top of these
models: a transcript a model will confidently defend is not evidence the model ever "meant" it. The story is
cheap. It was cheap for us too.

Full method, the question bank, every per-trial log, and the aggregation are in the

of reading that led to the untested cell.
