I made a small app called [Rical 1876](https://rical-1876.vercel.app). It is a timeline
of the late Ottoman and early Republican statesmen (the _ricâl-i devlet_), from the
Young Ottomans to the first years of the Republic. Each person is a vertical line and
time flows down. When people take part in the same event, their lines meet in a box, each
sitting with the side they were on. A line ends where the person dies.

The code is on GitHub: [CahidArda/rical-1876](https://github.com/CahidArda/rical-1876).

## Where it came from

I was in Macedonia recently, and I spent the trip reading about the events of the early
20th century: Resen, Manastır, 1908. Back home I kept thinking about Çerkes Hasan, the
officer who burst into a cabinet meeting in 1876, and about who he shot and why.

We hear these names all through history class: Midhat Paşa, Enver, Talât, Niyazi,
Mahmud Şevket Paşa. But since we didn't live back then, they feel like characters who
appear out of nowhere, do one thing and leave. Who were they really, once you put them in
context? Who stood next to whom, and who turned on whom later? This timeline is my answer
to that: the statesmen of the period, placed in relation to each other.

## How it is drawn

The page is a single HTML file with no build step: the data (people, events, who was on
which side) and the code that draws it as an SVG. The interesting part is the layout. A
chart like this turns into spaghetti fast, so I borrowed the method used for
"storyline" visualizations, the charts that show which characters of a film share a
scene:

- **Order the lines to avoid crossings.** Every event is a layer. In each layer the
  lines, the event and the parties inside it are sorted by the average position of the
  same people in the layer above, then again using the layer below, back and forth. This
  is the barycenter heuristic from layered graph drawing. After a few dozen sweeps the
  order with the fewest crossings wins.
- **Straighten them.** With the order fixed, every line should move sideways as little as
  possible while keeping a minimum gap from its neighbours and staying out of boxes it is
  not part of. In each layer that is a small least-squares problem with ordering
  constraints, which isotonic regression solves exactly. Iterating over the layers
  settles the whole picture.
- **Keep bystanders still.** A final pass keeps a line that is not part of an event
  exactly where it was, so it only bends when a box needs the room.

The lines carry the names, and tapping (or hovering over) a line, a box or a ✕ explains
it.

## What surprised me

How often it was the same people. The man who goes to the mountains in 1908 marches on
Istanbul in 1909, storms the Sublime Porte in 1913 and runs the war in 1914. The officer
sent off to Sofia in 1913, a polite way of getting him out of the capital, is the one
landing at Samsun in 1919. The opposition of 1912 is the government that loses the
Balkan War, and the man behind the 1876 constitution is later tried for the death of the
sultan he helped depose.

I had learned all of these events separately. Seeing who was who, and what they did,
laid out in one picture made them much easier to follow than any list of dates.
