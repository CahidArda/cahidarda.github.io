I have a 120-day French streak on Duolingo. I still can't speak French that well.

I haven't skipped a day. The problem is that what Duolingo asks you to do barely resembles using a language. Real life is not filling in the blank of a sentence you have already seen fifty times, choosing from a row of words where only one could possibly fit.

## Multiple choice without the choice

Take a typical exercise. The sentence is *Je ___ au marché* ("I ___ to the market"), and the options look something like this:

> **vais** · chat · rouge · demain

Only one of those is a verb, so all you need to know is what a verb looks like. A question that actually tests grammar would look more like this:

> **vais** · va · vont · allais

Now the only way to pick correctly is to know the conjugation. Duolingo mostly gives you the first kind of question. The correct option is obvious, you get it right, and the game moves on.

That's the problem in one sentence: **learning requires failing, and the app is built so you rarely fail.** Real learning means getting things wrong, spending time on why, and memorizing what you missed. Duolingo is optimized for something else: finishing lessons.

## The streak is the product

At 120 days, the streak isn't about French anymore. It's a chore. It's something I don't want to lose, so I keep going. It has become a self-propagating habit rather than a learning activity.

I'll probably keep the streak anyway. But I've stopped pretending it's how I'll learn French. And honestly, I don't think the standard ways of learning a language are ideal either. They work, but there's a better alternative, and I know because I've used it before, without meaning to.

## How I actually learned English

I took the usual English courses as a kid, and I can't claim they didn't help. But the biggest gains came by accident, from YouTube.

I wanted to understand how certain mechanics worked in the games I played. There wasn't content about them in Turkish, or I'd already consumed all of it and wanted more. So I searched in English. I played the games in English too: even when a Turkish translation existed, every guide and video I watched used the English terms, so it was simply easier to find things that way.

It wasn't only games. I've been watching [The Mighty Jingles](https://www.youtube.com/@TheMightyJingles) for about as long as I can remember, and he mostly plays games. [Lindy Beige](https://www.youtube.com/@LindyBeige) mostly doesn't. He doesn't upload often, but every video is a gem, and I've probably watched each of them at least twice.

For a long time I didn't understand half of what these people were saying. So I looked up the words I didn't know, one at a time, over years. My vocabulary grew enormously, and I never once sat down to "study English". English was just the toll I paid to reach content I actually wanted.

## Babies don't learn languages with textbooks

Nobody hands a toddler a conjugation table. Children pick up their first language from a huge amount of input they only partly understand, surrounded by context, with a strong reason to figure it out. The linguist Stephen Krashen built a whole theory of second-language learning around a similar idea, which he called *comprehensible input*: you acquire a language by understanding messages that are slightly beyond your current level.

It's an influential idea, and a debated one. Most researchers think output, feedback and deliberate practice matter too. And adults aren't babies. We have one enormous advantage over them: we already speak a language, and that language may be full of the one we're trying to learn.

## A goldmine hiding inside Turkish

Turkish has thousands of words borrowed from French. Look at older Ottoman vocabulary and you'll find even more. My favorite is the Ottoman word for railway, *şimendifer*, which is just French *chemin de fer*: literally, "road of iron".

If you're into etymology like I am, the French words Turks use every day are a free starter vocabulary:

| Turkish | French | What it literally means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| şimendifer | chemin de fer | road of iron (railway) |
| abajur | abat-jour | "knocks down the daylight" (lampshade) |
| randevu | rendez-vous | "present yourselves" (appointment) |
| şoför | chauffeur | "heater", originally the stoker on early steam cars |
| asansör | ascenseur | the thing that ascends (elevator) |
| kuaför | coiffeur | from *coiffe*, a headdress (hairdresser) |
| kuzen | cousin | cousin |

Once you know the French root, the Turkish word stops being arbitrary, and the French word stops being foreign. The same is true for English, which absorbed a huge share of its vocabulary from French after 1066. *Rendezvous* and *chauffeur* made the trip into English too.

There's an obvious limit. This works best when the languages you know share a lot of vocabulary with the one you're learning. An Arabic speaker learning Norwegian won't find much of a goldmine. Across French, English and Turkish, it works very well.

## So I'm building the YouTube part

The immersion half of this needs a tool, so I'm building one. You give it the name of a song or the URL of any YouTube video:

- **Songs:** it finds the lyrics in an open-source lyrics database and translates them with AI into your language, in my case French into English.
- **Any other video:** it pulls the transcript from YouTube and translates it the same way. A French vlog works exactly like a French song.

The video plays on the left. On the right, the French lines scroll along in sync, each with its English translation underneath. Hover over any French word and you see what it means on its own. Think Google Translate meets Duolingo, for videos. There's a quiz at the end, which is the least finished part and the one that makes it feel a bit like Duolingo, minus the obvious answers.

The plan is simple: find a French video I'd genuinely enjoy, drop it in, and learn French the way I learned English. Except this time, on purpose.

## Where this breaks: stages

I should be honest about a gap. When I see a Turkish or English word with a French root, I can usually guess what's going on, but that's because I already have a French baseline. I'm not sure the video-plus-etymology approach works for an absolute beginner. Understanding half of a video is useful. Understanding nothing isn't.

So I think the approach needs stages:

1. **Foundation.** Pronunciation, the most common words, core grammar. Yes, this is where a course, or even Duolingo, is actually useful. The irony isn't lost on me.
2. **Bridge.** Mine the loanwords you already know. It turns "French is a foreign language" into "I know hundreds of French words already", which is a much better mood to learn in.
3. **Immersion.** Videos and songs you'd watch anyway, with the app as scaffolding. This is where vocabulary grows fast.
4. **Output.** Actually speaking. None of the above fully solves this, and it's the thing my 120-day streak was supposed to fix. I'll write about it when I've figured it out.

## The streak continues

I'll keep the streak. Probably. The little flame has emotional leverage over me at this point. But I no longer expect it to make me speak French. That's going to come from a French vlog I actually want to watch, a hover over a word I didn't know, and the quiet satisfaction of noticing it was in Turkish all along.
