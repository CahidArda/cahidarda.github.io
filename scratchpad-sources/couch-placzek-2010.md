Recent literature reviews conclude that job displacement results in sustained earnings losses
(Bruce C. Fallick 1996; Lori G. Kletzer 1998) and that estimates of the size of those reductions
vary with the type of data used in the analysis, the industry within which displacement occurs,
and business cycle conditions. The largest estimated losses were obtained using administrative
data from Pennsylvania during the 1970s and 1980s (Louis S. Jacobson, Robert J. LaLonde, and
Daniel G. Sullivan 1993a). The data covered a period of high unemployment in a heavily industrialized state characterized by disproportionate job losses in manufacturing. Thus, the ability
to generalize those results to more favorable economic times and states with a greater reliance
on service sector employment has been questioned. To address these issues, administrative data
are used to similarly examine the impact of job displacement on earnings losses of workers in
Connecticut from 1993 to 2004.
The work of Jacobson, LaLonde, and Sullivan (JLS) also provided important methodological advancement by adapting analytical techniques developed for program evaluation (James
J. Heckman and V. Joseph Hotz 1989) to the context of job displacement. The same techniques
(fixed-effects and time trend estimators) as those used in the original JLS publication are initially
employed here. Then, the methods are extended to include matching estimators developed since
their publication. Both nearest neighbor estimators of Average Treatment on the Treated (ATT)
and Differenced Average Treatment on the Treated (DATT) are adapted to the context of this
study (Heckman, Hidehiko Ichimura, and Petra Todd 1997; Heckman et al. 1998; and Rajeev H.
Dehejia and Sadek Wahba 1999, 2002).
The results presented here for Connecticut differ in some ways from those found in the work
of JLS using data from Pennsylvania. In the period immediately following job loss, regardless
of the technique employed, earnings reductions for workers displaced through mass layoff range
from 32 percent to 33 percent; JLS reported immediate losses of more than 40 percent. Six
years later, using the same estimators as JLS, earnings reductions in Connecticut range from
13 percent to 15 percent; they report sustained losses in Pennsylvania of 25 percent. The smaller
long-term impacts in Connecticut demonstrate that under more ordinary economic times, estimated losses from administrative data lie within the range observed using other data sources.
This finding resolves a long-standing conflict among the results of high-quality studies that have
used differing data sources to study earnings reductions following job loss.1

I.  Prior Literature
Economists have many motivations for empirical studies of job displacement. There is a genuine interest in the economic difficulties that workers face when they have been employed on a
sustained basis and lose their jobs due to a reason beyond their control, such as a large-scale layoff or plant closure. Moreover, many view these large-scale events as a natural experiment that
allows researchers employing modern program evaluation methodologies to accurately gauge
the wage losses that result from breaking ties to a specific firm and losing the component of
wages associated with that employer. Evidence of long-term earnings losses after displacement
is indicative of the contribution of firm-specific factors to wage determination.4
The empirical literature on the earnings impact of job displacement is well established.5
Within those studies, there is consensus that displacement leads to sustained earnings losses;
however, the magnitude of those estimates vary according to the data source used, and whether
a comparison group is employed in the analysis, demographic groups are examined, or workers
switch industries to find postdisplacement reemployment, among other factors.

JLS (1993a, b) provide the only published studies of earnings losses following job displacement using administrative data that appear to be representative of most workers in a state, in
this case Pennsylvania.6
 They consider the impact of large-scale layoffs on long tenure workers
who had been employed continuously for at least six years with their current employer prior to
changing jobs. In this context, the term mass layoff is used rather than displacement since no
additional information is available in administrative data to assure that workers did not voluntarily separate from a firm.
The administrative data used in their analysis are drawn from earnings records that states
keep for the purpose of calculating UI benefits if a worker loses employment. They match these
individual level records to data from the Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages (QCEW),
which are initially collected for the purpose of calculating employer UI tax liability and are
enhanced for use in producing statistical reports. The advantage of these administrative records
is that earnings are recorded from firm payrolls on a quarterly basis so they are more reliable
than any survey measure that requires individual recall.
JLS (1993a, b) report earnings losses for workers age 20 to 50 of more than 40 percent the
year following a mass layoff. Six years after the original job is lost, earnings are still reduced by
25 percent. For workers who separate from their jobs and are not part of a mass layoff, they find
initial losses of 26 percent with no deficit observed six years later. They examine UI recipients
among all job separators and report initial earnings losses of 66 percent and reductions of 24
percent six years later. These calculations apply to those workers who are observed reemployed.
The JLS study is important. It introduced the use of program evaluation techniques into the
job displacement literature. The use of administrative earnings records from the UI system of
Pennsylvania in their study also reduces concerns about recall bias, and, based on those data,
they find the largest estimates of earnings losses associated with displacement in the literature.
While the authors argue that their findings are due to the superior quality of the data used in the
study and the focus of the research on long-tenure workers, reasonable doubts exist about the
scale of the estimates due to location and timing.

energy prices, import penetration, and declines in domestic demand for traditional manufacturing products.7

group, they are found to have initial earnings losses of 47 percent. Six years later those losses are
22 percent. This is similar to the pattern reported by JLS (2005a) for Washington.

An alternative method to control for individual heterogeneity is to choose a continuously
employed worker who resembles each separator and to use those pairs to calculate earnings
losses.11 The idea of matching on observable characteristics has been previously explored in the
program evaluation literature (LaLonde 1986; Thomas Fraker and Rebecca Maynard 1987). The
problem that often arises is that in grouping the potential comparisons into bins based on their
observable characteristics, finding exact matches for the separators may not be possible.12
Paul R. Rosenbaum and Donald B. Rubin (1983) reduce the dimensionality of this problem by
suggesting that matches be based on the predicted probability of the event (mass layoff) associated with the outcome (earnings) being studied. An advantage of this approach is that individuals
who differ in some areas still may be close matches based on their propensity to experience an
event.

would be biased. However, stability of estimates across different procedures would be consistent

III.  Data
The data used in this study are drawn from state administrative files from Connecticut. The
UI wage file contains a code identifying employers of each individual.13 Those codes are used to
match the wage data to firm information from the QCEW. Further, the wage files contain social
security numbers, which are used to link them to Connecticut Department of Motor Vehicle
records in order to obtain demographic information. As in the original JLS study, all individuals
in the sample must report positive wages the first quarter of the study period and in each following year.14 The resulting file contains information on 61 percent of all workers in Connecticut.
The only Connecticut workers systematically excluded from the analysis are those who commute
to the state for work; thus, the matches and the study are representative of the resident worker

Employment separations in the study are identified by tracking changes in employment
ID numbers (EINs). The validity of the coding over time is important. Internal files from the
Connecticut Department of Labor (DOL) regarding the reason for the change in coding were
used to determine whether the lapsing of a particular employer identification number was due to
plant closure or not. The Connecticut DOL keeps information on these changes in order to correctly calculate UI risk ratings.16
In the simplest case, an individual has a sole employer in a particular quarter and is observed
working for a different firm the following period. This is considered a job separation in this study
if it occurs after 1998. If the individual’s change in employment occurred within a year (before
or after) of a drop in the firm’s employment to 30 percent or more below its maximum level prior
to 1999, it is considered a displacement due to mass layoff. When calculating these employment
changes, the figures are volatile for small employers. For this reason, those working for employers with fewer than 50 employees are removed from the sample.
There are individuals in the sample with multiple employers, and the changes between them
do not always progress in a smooth manner. If there is a transition from one employer to another,
the change is assumed to occur in the last quarter in which the code of the prior job is observed.
The separation is coded at that time and the determination is made of whether this change was
associated with a mass layoff event. Web Appendices A through E contain more information on
data construction.
The primary purpose of looking at workers from firms where employment has declined by
at least 30 percent is to reduce the likelihood that voluntary leavers and workers fired for cause
are included in the sample. However, other rules might be considered, such as examining UI
recipients, since this would remove those fired for cause and job quitters from the sample, or
alternatively, including only workers from firms that close. Changes in the rules used to select the
group of job separators examined influence the depth of estimated earnings losses. After presenting estimates of earnings loss that employ the sample selection rules of JLS (1993a), a series of
alternative approaches to selecting the mass layoff sample is considered.
IV.  Descriptive Information and Parameter Estimates
A table with descriptive information on the 1998 sample, the last year before job separations,
is in Web Appendix F. A total of 95,126 individuals meet screening criteria for the sample. Of

V.  Subgroup Variations
In order to investigate whether earnings losses due to mass layoffs in Connecticut varied in
dimensions known to be correlated with differing levels of specific skills, estimates of equation
(2) including other available variables were also calculated.26 For each variable, a spline was
formed as described in the methodology section. The spline is first estimated for each variable
individually and then including all of the other regressors. The parameter estimates are used to
calculate earnings losses the fifth year after job loss. The table of results is contained in Web
Appendix G. With few exceptions, the ordering of the estimated impacts is similar across the two
sets of estimates, so only those containing all variables are considered here.
Previous research has investigated the pattern of earnings losses across industries, since manufacturing workers, in particular, are thought to develop more specific skills than others and
would be expected to have larger than average earnings losses when they separate from employment. In the Connecticut data, workers in finance, insurance, and real estate ($13,450) and business and professional services ($12,093) have the largest earnings reductions the fifth year after
job loss. Losses among manufacturing workers are third largest ($11,108). Earnings for workers
in education and health services have fully recovered five years after job loss ($2,021). The finding that highly paid service workers have larger average earnings reductions in the long term
than manufacturing workers in the Connecticut economy suggests that contemporary research
on the nature of general and specific skills within services is needed.
Older workers are also expected to experience larger earnings losses following mass layoff
than others because of their greater employment tenure. In the Connecticut data, workers born in
the 1950s have annual earnings reductions five years after job loss ($10,225), more than double
those of workers born in the 1970s ($4,098). Also, reductions for women ($7,367) five years
after job loss are similar to those of men ($8,694), but since women’s preseparation earnings are
smaller, the percentage reductions for them are larger.
Past research (Carrington 1993; Neal 1995) has also investigated the importance of reemployment in the same specific industry or in the broader industry group versus transitioning to
another sector in terms of the depth of earnings losses associated with displacement. A table in
Web Appendix H contains estimates of the earnings losses of workers reemployed in the same
six-digit NAICS, reemployed outside the identical NAICS code but within the broad industry
aggregate, and for those who move across major industry groupings.
In both manufacturing and nonmanufacturing, those reemployed in a firm with the same
NAICS code have smaller earnings losses; those who are reemployed in a firm in the same sector
have larger losses; and those who move outside the sector have the largest losses. The primary
difference across industry groupings is that earnings losses for manufacturing workers reemployed in a different sector are larger than for nonmanufacturing workers reemployed in a different sector. This suggests that, on average, firm- and industry-specific factors are more important
in determining the earnings of manufacturing workers.

JLS also wrote two papers examining community college (CC) training of workers who experienced mass layoff in Washington and filed UI claims (2005a, b). They plot earnings paths of
workers who experience mass layoffs and received UI benefits, stratifying the sample based on
subsequent CC attendance (2005a, figure 1). Earnings reductions at the time of job loss relative
to the peak level in the figure are 43 percent and 66 percent, respectively, for UI claimants who
do and do not attend CC. Respective losses four years later are 29 percent and 33 percent. When
comparable before and after comparisons are calculated for UI recipients in Connecticut, earnings losses at the time of job separation are 47 percent and 22 percent six years later.
The rule used to determine whether a worker was included in the mass layoff sample compares the current quarterly employment of their firm relative to its maximum level in the period
from 1993 to 1998. When workers lose their job within a year of a reduction in employment of
30 percent or more, they are included in the mass layoff sample. One implication is that even if
a firm has a very gradual drop in employment over a long period of time, all workers who leave
the firm after its employment level declines by 30 percent are considered to have experienced a
mass layoff. Altering the rule to capture different types of drops in employment will change both
the group of firms that have a mass layoff event and the affected workers.
To test the sensitivity of the estimates for the mass layoff sample in this paper to alternative
methods of selection, four alternative rules were examined.28 When larger drops in employment
relative to the maximum prior to 1999 and a quarter-to-quarter drop in employment of 30 percent
or more were examined, the long-term earnings losses did not vary meaningfully from those
shown in the paper. When all workers who separated from firms that close were examined, longterm losses were 3 percent larger.29
One alteration in the rule used to select the mass layoff sample that results in noticeably
larger estimates gauges the drop in firm employment relative to its level in 1998:IV. The mass
layoff sample becomes smaller, consisting of 11,226 individuals, and long-term losses using
the fixed-effects and time trend estimators increase to 16 percent and 19 percent, respectively.
While these losses are noticeably larger, the associated standard errors indicate that they are not
significantly different from the estimates obtained using the primary mass layoff selection rule
employed in the paper.30 Nonetheless, aggregated earnings losses for a typical worker in the mass
layoff sample under the alternative rule are 15 percent larger in postdisplacement periods (using
the time trend estimates). This suggests that reductions in employment that occur over longer
time frames result in smaller costs to individual workers.

These estimates are similar in magnitude to those reported by Ruhm (1991) and Stevens (1997),

1997), who used the DWS. Thus, the larger sustained losses reported by JLS appear to be related
to circumstances in Pennsylvania rather than to properties of administrative data in general.
The original estimation methodologies of JLS (1993a, b) were also extended to matching
estimators developed since the time of their original studies. Propensity score estimators that
calculate the average difference in earnings across matched pairs of similar individuals (ATT) as
well as the average difference-in-differences (DATT) were employed in the study. Using each of
those estimators, similar earnings losses are found at the time of mass layoff (33 percent) and six
years later (12 percent). The largest long-term loss based on comparisons with all continuously
employed workers using equations (1) and (2) was 15 percent. While this difference in results
across the types of estimators is not large, it does indicate that the fixed-effects and time trend
estimators may somewhat overstate the impact of mass layoff on earnings due to sample selection. For the mass layoff sample, however, associated standard errors indicate that the estimated
losses across techniques are not meaningfully different from each other.
While average earnings losses among workers displaced by mass layoff are smaller in
Connecticut than in Pennsylvania, an important common finding in both samples is that those
losses are highly related to the depth of earnings losses among UI recipients. Indeed, the depth
of the earnings losses among those separators who received UI benefits in Connecticut was
larger than in Pennsylvania. The reason that average estimated long-term losses were smaller in
Connecticut is because the incidence of benefit receipt was much lower than in Pennsylvania.
Further, in the Connecticut data, the size of long-term losses increases with the duration of
benefit receipt. Average educational levels have risen and job tenure has fallen in the United
States since the time period examined by the Pennsylvania studies. Higher education attainment
reduces the chance of drawing UI benefits, and shorter tenure is related to smaller long-term
losses. Thus, each of these underlying trends is likely to have contributed to the smaller incidence
of benefit receipt in the Connecticut data, and perhaps to shorter durations as well. The observation that a high incidence of UI receipt in the Pennsylvania data drove the relatively large earnings losses estimated with those data also provides a reasonable explanation of why those results
differed from those obtained from research that made use of the DWS and PSID. Thus, studies
aimed directly at better understanding the factors that lead to workers applying for UI following
mass layoffs—and designing policies that move those workers more rapidly to reemployment—
should be priorities for future research.
