# Robots vs. AI: Source Document & Blog Kit

A research brief for a blog post comparing the automation of production lines (industrial robots, ~1990–2015) with the current wave of AI displacing knowledge workers. Contains: (1) sources with findings and citable numbers, (2) a blog outline, (3) diagram specs.

**Core thesis to anchor the post:** The factory-robot story is a real base rate for what displacement looks like — concentrated, geographically brutal, permanent for a minority, poorly cushioned by retraining. But the distributional shape is close to *inverted*: robots climbed *down* the education ladder (hit dropouts hardest), while AI exposure climbs *up* it (rises with income/skill). So the analogy is useful for the *mechanics* of displacement and useless for predicting *who* gets hit.

## Part 1 — Sources & Findings

### A. The magnitude of robot displacement

**Acemoglu, D. & Restrepo, P. (2020). "Robots and Jobs: Evidence from US Labor Markets." *Journal of Political Economy* 128(6). (NBER WP 23285, 2017.)**
The canonical study. US commuting zones, 1990–2007.
- One additional robot per 1,000 workers reduced the local employment-to-population ratio by ~0.2 percentage points and wages by ~0.42%.
- Larger effects within the most exposed local markets: ~0.39pp employment and ~0.77% wages.
- On average, one new industrial robot coincided with ~5.6 fewer workers in the local labor market.
- Hit was occupation-specific: routine manual jobs — machinists, assemblers, material handlers, welders.
- All education levels were hurt, but workers **without** a college degree were hit far more. Notably, robots showed **no** positive complementarity for workers with master's/advanced degrees — unlike earlier IT waves.
- Geographically concentrated: Detroit was the single most exposed commuting zone; parts of Texas hit 5–10 robots per 1,000 workers.
- Aggregate effect on total US employment was still a modest share — this is not a "most jobs vanish" story.
- URL: https://www.nber.org/papers/w23285 · https://shapingwork.mit.edu/research/robots-and-jobs-evidence-from-us-labor-markets/

**CEPR / VoxEU summary of the above (scenario projection).**
- Under aggressive adoption scenarios, implied ~0.94–1.76pp lower employment-to-population ratio and ~1.3–2.6% lower wage growth for 2015–2025 — "sizable" but still a small fraction of total employment.
- URL: https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/robots-and-jobs-evidence-us

### B. Cross-country: the US result does NOT cleanly generalize

**Graetz, G. & Michaels, G. (2018).** Cross-country panel: industrial robots raised productivity and wages but reduced low-skill employment. More optimistic than the US result.

**Dauth, W., Findeisen, S., Südekum, J. & Wößner, N. ("German Robots," IAB 2017/2021).** German labor markets 1994–2014: robots caused **no net job loss**; they reshaped job composition. Negative effects were confined to manufacturing.
- Takeaway for the blog: the sharp negative displacement story is most robustly a *US* story, plausibly mediated by weaker safety nets and labor institutions.

### C. Who gets hit — the inverted distribution (the key AI contrast)

**"Impact of robots and AI on labor and skill demand: evidence from the UK." *Eurasian Business Review* (2025).**
- Robot exposure is **strongest among high-school dropouts and declines monotonically with education**.
- AI exposure **increases monotonically across income percentiles** — i.e., rises with skill/income.
- Robots concentrated on the **middle** of the income distribution; AI exposure climbs toward the top.
- URL: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40821-025-00314-w

**Acemoglu & Restrepo (2020) + Autor (2024), as summarized in the AI-exposure-index paper (arXiv:2510.13369).**
- Robots are tangible, rivalrous assets; AI is intangible and non-rivalrous — so AI may affect wages/employment differently. Explicit warning against "false analogies."
- **Autor (2024) hypothesizes AI may *reverse* the wage polarization that robots/IT caused** — the single most blog-relevant strand for a software/design audience.
- URL: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.13369

**St. Louis Fed (2024), "Robots: Helpers or Substitutes?" + The Century Foundation (2022).**
- Worst wage effects landed on young, less-educated Black men and women in Midwest manufacturing.
- Older workers adapted worst (training-access gaps).
- URLs: https://www.stlouisfed.org/open-vault/2024/sep/are-robots-helpers-or-substitutes-for-workers · https://tcf.org/content/report/robots-beginning-affect-workers-wages/

### D. What happened to displaced workers afterward (the base rate)

*Note: this literature is mass-layoff / displacement broadly, not robot-specific — flag that in the post.*

**Jacobson, LaLonde & Sullivan (1993).** 1980s high-seniority displaced workers: ~**30% initial earnings loss**; still ~**20% below** non-displaced peers **15–20 years later**.

**Couch & Placzek (2010).** ~**33% immediate** earnings loss; still ~**15% down six years** out. Cumulative lifetime earnings loss ~20%, with wage "scarring" visible up to 20 years post-displacement (also von Wachter et al.).

**BLS Displaced Workers Survey (January 2024).**
- **65.7%** of long-tenured displaced workers were reemployed.
- Of full-time reemployed, **62% earned as much or more** than the lost job — meaning **~38% took a permanent pay cut**.
- Manufacturing was 17% of long-tenured displacements.
- URL: https://www.bls.gov/news.release/disp.nr0.htm

**Chicago Fed (2004).** 2001–2003: 5.3M workers displaced, ~1/3 from manufacturing; ~65% reemployed within roughly a year.

**"The Far-Reaching Impact of Job Loss and Unemployment" (PMC).** Displacement linked to persistent earnings loss, declines in physical/psychological well-being, family disruption, higher divorce rates, and worse child outcomes. Reemployment mitigates but does not erase these.
- URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4553243/

**The key mechanism:** most of the lifetime cost comes not from a lower wage on the next job but from **difficulty finding stable full-time work** — longer non-employment spells and more part-time landings.

### E. The political / social aftermath

**Yildirim, Petrova, Schubert et al. "Automation, Career Values, and Political Preferences."**
- One robot per 1,000 workers cut local average "career value" by ~**$3,900 (2004–2008)** and ~$2,480 (2008–2016).
- Automation-hit regions cut long-term investment in housing/education and shifted toward **populist / GOP candidates in 2016**.
- URL: https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/robots-are-taking-over-low-skilled-jobs-and-changing-votes/

### F. Did workers resist? (Mostly bargained over terms — and lost)

- **Luddites (1810s)** — the deep root. Skilled textile workers destroying frames not from technophobia but because mechanization destroyed their *bargaining position*. Same structure, 200 years early.
- **ILWU Mechanization & Modernization Agreement (1960)** — dockworkers **accepted** containerization for benefits; created a two-tier "A-men / B-men" workforce that foreshadowed modern two-tier labor. (Marc Levinson, *The Box*, 2006, on how containerization eliminated longshore work.)
- **East Coast longshore strike (1964)** — rank-and-file strike closed ports 18 days; forced LBJ to invoke Taft-Hartley; leadership settled for reduced gang sizes anyway.
- **UMW (1950s)** — John L. Lewis backed the continuous miner expecting secure, better-paid jobs; it did not deliver.
- **UAW** — accepted automation for job security through mid-century; concessions began late 1970s.
- **Modern echo:** Teamsters/UPS contract (2023) banned driverless trucks for its duration; **ILA (2024)** threatened to shut East/Gulf ports demanding a **total ban** on automating cranes, gates, container moves — framing full automation as existential, not tradeable.
- **EPI "Zombie Robot" argument** — "automation causes joblessness" is a myth; the real variable is **worker bargaining power** over the terms of transition. (Labor-aligned reading; weigh against Acemoglu's evidence that displacement was real regardless.)
- URLs: https://libcom.org/article/effects-automation-lives-longshoremen · https://www.city-journal.org/article/international-longshoremens-association-dockworkers-ports-trade-technology-unions · https://govfacts.org/money/work-workplace-issues/unions-collective-bargaining/how-american-unions-have-changed-since-the-1930s/

### G. How companies & policy responded

- Firms chose among three moves — **automate, offshore, or both** — often substituting between them. Research on the "robots–reshoring nexus" finds reshoring to high-wage countries went hand-in-hand with *more automation*, not job creation. "The jobs came back" ≠ "the jobs came back to people."
- **Trade Adjustment Assistance (TAA), 1962–2022** — extended income support, wage subsidies, relocation allowances, retraining. One study: participants ~**$50,000 higher cumulative earnings after 10 years**, but the benefit **dissipated over time**. Retraining worked **only** when bundled with "wraparound supports" (job-search help, counseling, placement).
- **IMF finding:** US regions with **more generous unemployment benefits (2000–2007)** had **smaller** automation-driven wage losses — the safety net cushioned more than retraining did.
- URL: https://bipartisanpolicy.org/explainer/what-happens-when-jobs-disappear-a-guide-to-displaced-worker-programs-in-the-u-s/

## Part 2 — Blog Outline

**Working title options:**
- "What Factory Robots Already Told Us About AI and Your Job"
- "The Robot Playbook: What 25 Years of Factory Automation Predicts (and Doesn't) About AI"
- "Robots Went Down the Ladder. AI Is Coming Up It."

**Hook (opening, ~150 words):** Open on the mirror image. Everyone drawing the robots→AI parallel assumes it transfers cleanly. It transfers on *mechanics* and inverts on *targets*. State the thesis up front. → *Diagram 1 sits here.*

**Section 1 — The base rate: what robots actually did.**
The Acemoglu–Restrepo numbers (5.6 workers per robot; 0.2pp / 0.42%). Occupations hit. Geographic concentration (Detroit). Frame it as: this is the most rigorous natural experiment we have for "a technology directly substitutes for labor at scale." → *Diagram 2.*

**Section 2 — The uncomfortable part: displacement is permanent for a minority.**
The scarring literature — 30% → 20% over 15–20 years. The 62%/38% reemployment split. The real mechanism (non-employment spells, not lower wages). Social spillovers (well-being, family, politics). → *Diagram 3 + Diagram 4.*

**Section 3 — Where the analogy breaks: the inverted distribution.**
Robots hit dropouts and the middle; AI exposure rises with income. Autor's "AI may reverse polarization" hypothesis. This is the section your software/design readers actually care about — spell out what it means for credentialed knowledge workers. → *Diagram 1 callback / expanded.*

**Section 4 — Did anyone fight it? (And should they?)**
Luddites → M&M agreement → 1964 strike → UPS/ILA today. The pattern: labor bargained over *terms*, rarely stopped the tech, and did best when it had power, not when it had retraining. The EPI "bargaining power, not robots" argument vs. the Acemoglu "displacement was real anyway" evidence — present both. → *Diagram 5 (timeline).*

**Section 5 — What companies and governments did — and what worked.**
Automate/offshore/both. TAA's mixed record. The counterintuitive punchline: **safety nets cushioned better than reskilling did.** Draw the implication for AI policy debates.

**Conclusion — The one asymmetry that matters.**
Robots hit a group with weak political voice but strong union structure. AI is hitting a group with strong political voice but almost no union structure. That inversion — not the technology — is what will decide how this wave plays out. End on the open question rather than a prediction.

**Credibility note to include:** flag that the displaced-worker scarring data is mass-layoff-wide, not robot-specific, and that the German/cross-country evidence complicates the US story. Honesty here buys trust with a technical audience.

## Part 3 — Diagram Specs

### Diagram 1 — "The Inverted Ladder" (the signature visual)
- **Type:** Two-line chart, shared x-axis.
- **X-axis:** education / income percentile, low → high (left to right).
- **Y-axis:** relative exposure to automation (index).
- **Series A (Robots):** downward-sloping line — highest exposure at "high-school dropout," declining monotonically to lowest at "advanced degree."
- **Series B (AI):** upward-sloping line — rising monotonically across income percentiles.
- **The point:** the two lines cross. Annotate the crossover. This one image carries the whole thesis.
- **Source:** Eurasian Business Review (2025); Acemoglu–Restrepo; Autor (2024).

### Diagram 2 — "One Robot, 5.6 Jobs"
- **Type:** Icon/unit chart or small waterfall.
- **Content:** one robot icon → 5.6 worker icons removed; sidebar stats "-0.2pp employment / -0.42% wages per robot/1,000 workers." Optionally a mini US map inset highlighting Detroit + Texas exposure hot zones.
- **The point:** make the abstract coefficient physical and concrete.
- **Source:** Acemoglu–Restrepo (2020).

### Diagram 3 — "The Scar Doesn't Heal"
- **Type:** Line chart.
- **X-axis:** years since displacement (0 → 20).
- **Y-axis:** earnings relative to non-displaced peers (%, negative).
- **Line:** starts at ~-30% at year 0, recovers to and plateaus around -20% by years 15–20. Optionally a second, shallower line for the Couch–Placzek path (-33% → -15% at year 6).
- **The point:** displacement is not a temporary dip; it's a permanent step-down for many.
- **Source:** Jacobson–LaLonde–Sullivan (1993); Couch–Placzek (2010).

### Diagram 4 — "Where Displaced Workers Landed"
- **Type:** Stacked horizontal bar (or simple Sankey).
- **Segments:** reemployed at equal-or-higher pay (~62% of reemployed) · reemployed at a pay cut (~38%) · not reemployed (~34% of the displaced pool). Be explicit in the caption about which denominator each figure uses — don't blur the two.
- **The point:** "reemployment" hides a large permanent-pay-cut tail.
- **Source:** BLS Displaced Workers Survey (Jan 2024).

### Diagram 5 — "200 Years of Bargaining, Not Stopping"
- **Type:** Horizontal timeline.
- **Nodes:** Luddites (1810s) → ILWU M&M Agreement (1960) → East Coast longshore strike (1964) → UAW automation-for-security era → Teamsters/UPS driverless-truck ban (2023) → ILA total-automation-ban demand (2024).
- **Encoding:** color or icon each node by outcome — "accepted for terms" vs. "resisted outright" vs. "struck." Shows the recurring pattern and the recent hardening of resistance.
- **The point:** resistance shaped terms; it rarely stopped the technology.
- **Source:** Section F above.

### Optional Diagram 6 — "The Cushion That Worked"
- **Type:** Scatter with fit line.
- **X-axis:** unemployment-benefit generosity by region. **Y-axis:** automation-driven wage loss (smaller = higher up).
- **The point:** safety nets cushioned displacement better than retraining did — the policy punchline.
- **Source:** IMF, via Bipartisan Policy Center (2025).
